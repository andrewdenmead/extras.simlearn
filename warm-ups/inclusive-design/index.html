Inclusive design

Who is a design really for, and who gets a say in shaping it? Pick a docket with your neighbour and talk through all four prompts.

Pick a docket with your neighbours and talk through its four questions. Explain your reasons, and ask what someone who disagrees would say.

All eight dockets

Docket 1

  1. Think of something that clearly wasn't designed for someone like you: too high, too small, wrong language, wrong hand. How did it feel?
  2. Lowered kerbs were made for wheelchairs but help parents with buggies, cyclists and travellers with suitcases too. Why don't designers start with these users more often?
  3. A ticket app for the BVG: one version that works for everyone, or separate versions for different needs? Which would you design, and why?
  4. Should public buildings have to be fully accessible, even historic ones where lifts and ramps would change how they look? Who should decide?

Docket 2

  1. Have you ever helped someone use technology they found difficult, like a grandparent or a neighbour? What was hardest for them?
  2. Who knows more about a wheelchair: the engineer who designed it, or someone who has used one for twenty years? What does each know that the other doesn't?
  3. Your budget covers subtitles or sign-language interpreting for an online event, not both. Which would you choose, and who would each leave out?
  4. Inclusive design often costs more at the start. Who should pay that extra cost: companies, customers or governments?

Docket 3

  1. Which everyday object do you think works brilliantly for almost everyone? What makes it so easy?
  2. Is it possible to design for 'everyone', or does designing for everyone mean designing for no one in particular?
  3. The HTW website could be redesigned for students with dyslexia: plain fonts, short texts, fewer pictures. Would other students gain or lose, and why?
  4. Voice assistants often struggle with strong accents; Scottish and Bavarian users regularly complain. Whose fault is that, and what should companies do?

Docket 4

  1. Do you ever use subtitles, dark mode or large text even when you don't 'need' them? Why?
  2. Many websites are hard to use with a screen reader. Should accessibility be the law for every website, or only for public services?
  3. Small budget: make your app fully accessible now, or launch sooner and fix accessibility later? What would each choice cost, and who would pay?
  4. When people take part in design research, should they be paid like consultants? What would change if they were?

Docket 5

  1. Think of a building at HTW or in Berlin that is hard to get around. Who has the hardest time there?
  2. Self-checkouts save shops money but confuse some customers. Should shops always keep a staffed till open, even if it costs more? Who should decide?
  3. A train company can add more priority seats, leaving fewer for everyone else. Where would you draw the line, and who would complain?
  4. Should companies be allowed to call a product 'inclusive' in their adverts? What should they have to prove first?

Docket 6

  1. What's the most frustrating form you've ever filled in, online or on paper? Who do you think it was designed for?
  2. Left-handed people still struggle with scissors, notebooks and desks. Why has the design world fixed some exclusions faster than others?
  3. A game studio can add lots of difficulty and accessibility settings, or keep the game 'as the creator intended'. Which would you choose?
  4. When designers create something for a group they don't belong to, how can they avoid getting it wrong? Are good intentions enough?

Docket 7

  1. Is public transport in Berlin designed well for everyone? Think of lifts, announcements and ticket machines.
  2. Car crash-test dummies were based on an average man for decades. What other designs might be built around an 'average' person, and who loses?
  3. A public website: simple enough for everyone, or rich in features for expert users? Which would you prioritise, and why?
  4. Should every design student have to design with disabled people as part of their degree? What would they gain, and what would critics say?

Docket 8

  1. Have you ever been somewhere you couldn't read the signs or the menu? How did the design help you, or not?
  2. Is inclusive design mainly about disability, or also about age, language, income and culture? Where would you draw its edges?
  3. A café: lively music and a buzzy atmosphere, or a calm space for people who find noise difficult? Which would you design, and who loses?
  4. A new playground could make every piece usable by every child, even if that means fewer exciting, risky things to climb. Who decides?
