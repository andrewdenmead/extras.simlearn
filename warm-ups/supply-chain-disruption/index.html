Warm-up · EIM · C1

Supply chain disruption

Almost everything we buy travels a long way, and one small break can stop it. Pick a docket with your neighbour and talk through all four prompts.

Pick a docket with your neighbours and talk through its four questions. Explain your reasons, and ask what someone who disagrees would say.

All eight dockets

Docket 1

  1. Before recent crises, how often did you think about where the things you buy come from? What made you start noticing?
  2. If one blocked canal can disrupt thousands of companies, has globalisation become too dependent on a few routes? What would the alternative cost?
  3. When shipping costs suddenly rise, who should pay: the manufacturer, the shop or the customer? Which would you choose if you ran the shop?
  4. What is worse for a manager: spending too much preparing for a crisis that never happens, or too little and being caught unprepared? What would shareholders say?

Docket 2

  1. Have you ever had to wait much longer than expected for something you'd ordered? What did you do instead?
  2. Is 'just-in-time' production clever efficiency, or a bet that nothing will go wrong? What would a factory manager say?
  3. In a crisis with too little stock, which customer gets supplied first: the biggest, the most loyal, or the one who would suffer most? Defend your choice.
  4. If climate change makes floods and storms more common, can companies still call supply-chain failures 'unexpected'? Who should carry the risk?

Docket 3

  1. During a shortage you remember, such as toilet paper, flour or computer chips, what couldn't you buy? What did it teach you about how fragile supply is?
  2. Should a company tell customers straight away when deliveries may be late, or wait until it knows how serious the problem is? What could go wrong either way?
  3. Would you rather your local Späti stocked a few products that are always available, or many products that sometimes run out? Why?
  4. Many governments now want key products like chips and medicines made at home again. Is that sensible protection or expensive nationalism? What would consumers say?

Docket 4

  1. Think of a time you planned something carefully and one small thing ruined it. Which single link failed?
  2. Why do companies cut 'just in case' costs in good times, even though everyone knows crises happen? Who is to blame?
  3. Deutsche Bahn is often late. Would you rather it ran fewer trains reliably, or more trains with frequent delays? Who benefits from each?
  4. Fast, cheap delivery is now normal for online shopping. Who really pays for it: workers, the environment or other businesses? What would a customer say?

Docket 5

  1. Which everyday product you use has probably travelled the furthest to reach you? Guess its route.
  2. Is it fair for a company to raise prices during a crisis because its costs went up, or does that become profiteering? Where's the line?
  3. Would you rather keep extra essentials at home 'just in case', or trust shops always to have them? What does your choice say about you?
  4. A supplier in a poorer country can't deliver after a natural disaster. Should a European buyer switch suppliers, or stand by them? What would each side say?

Docket 6

  1. Have you ever had to cover for someone at work or in a group project at the last minute? How did you cope?
  2. Many shops promise next-day delivery. Is that realistic in today's world, or are customers being trained to expect too much?
  3. Would you rather pay a little more for everything so companies keep big safety stocks, or pay less and accept occasional empty shelves? Defend your choice.
  4. Container ships, lorries and planes create huge emissions. In a crisis, should keeping deliveries moving come before climate goals? What would a climate activist say?

Docket 7

  1. Which product would you be surprised to learn is made in only one or two factories in the world?
  2. When a delivery goes wrong, customers usually blame the shop, not the shipping company. Is that fair? What could the shop do differently?
  3. Would you rather work in a role that prevents crises nobody ever sees, or one that solves visible crises and gets the credit? Which do companies reward more?
  4. Should rich countries help poorer countries build their own factories, so the world depends less on a few places? Who would benefit and who would lose?

Docket 8

  1. Scotch whisky can only be made in Scotland, yet most of it is sold abroad. Think of a product from your region that travels far. What could disrupt its journey?
  2. Supermarkets like Lidl promise low prices all year. What do you think happens behind the scenes when costs suddenly jump?
  3. Would you rather your company depended on one excellent supplier, or three average ones? What happens to quality, price and relationships in each case?
  4. When a crisis costs a company money, should shareholders, staff or customers feel it first? Which would be fairest, and which most realistic?
