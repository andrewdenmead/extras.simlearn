Protest and policing

The right to protest is easy to defend in theory and harder in a traffic jam. Pick a docket, talk it through with your neighbour, and give reasons for your views.

Pick a docket with your neighbours and talk through its four questions. Explain your reasons, and ask what someone who disagrees would say.

All eight dockets

Docket 1

  1. Have you ever been to a protest or demonstration? What was it about, and how did it feel to be there?
  2. Climate activists in Germany glued themselves to roads in 2022 and 2023. Did that help their cause or harm it? Why?
  3. A protest that blocks traffic and gets noticed, or one that disturbs nobody and gets ignored: which would you join, and why?
  4. How much disruption should the public accept for other people's right to protest? What would someone stuck in the traffic say?

Docket 2

  1. Have you ever been held up by a protest, a strike or a road closure? How did you feel about the cause afterwards?
  2. Why do some protests change laws while others change nothing?
  3. A rail strike that cancels your trip home, or no strikes and lower pay for train drivers: which would you rather live with?
  4. Is the right to protest more important than the right to get to work? How would you decide between them in a real case?

Docket 3

  1. What's the biggest crowd you've ever been part of, at a match, a concert or a march? How was it managed?
  2. Why are police so visible at some events, like football matches, and almost absent at others?
  3. Officers in riot gear, or officers in ordinary uniform who chat with the crowd: which keeps a protest calmer, and why?
  4. Can a protest be legitimate if it breaks the law? Give an example, and say what a critic would answer.

Docket 4

  1. Which cause, if any, would get you out onto the streets? What would make you stay at home?
  2. Has social media made protests stronger or weaker? Why?
  3. Thousands marching through the city, or an online petition with a million signatures: which has more power today, and why?
  4. Should protesters be allowed to cover their faces? Who benefits, and what would the police say?

Docket 5

  1. Has a protest you've seen, in person or online, ever changed your mind about something? Which one, and how?
  2. Why do people trust the police so differently, depending on who they are and where they live?
  3. Strict rules on where and when protests can happen, or freedom to protest anywhere with more disruption: which would you choose for Berlin?
  4. Who should decide when a protest has gone too far: the police, a court, politicians or the public? Why?

Docket 6

  1. Which protest from history do you admire? What made it work?
  2. Are Berlin's May Day demonstrations a celebration, a protest or a problem? Why?
  3. A climate protest at an airport, or one outside a ministry: which is more effective, and which is fairer?
  4. Should a protest be judged by its methods or by its cause? What would someone who disagrees with you say?

Docket 7

  1. How do you usually show you disagree with a decision that affects you: complain, sign a petition, protest or stay quiet?
  2. Is a protest still a protest if it needs permission from the authorities? Why?
  3. A country with frequent, noisy protests, or one with almost none: which would you rather live in, and what does each tell you?
  4. Many countries are tightening their protest laws. Is that a sensible response to disruption, or a slow danger to democracy? What would the other side say?

Docket 8

  1. What's the most effective way you've seen someone change a decision at university or work: protest, petition or quiet persuasion?
  2. Why do some governments feel threatened by protests, even small ones?
  3. A march through the city centre on a Saturday, or a rally in a park on a weekday: which would you choose as an organiser, and why?
  4. Should strikes and protests by essential workers, like train drivers or nurses, be limited? Who wins and who loses?
