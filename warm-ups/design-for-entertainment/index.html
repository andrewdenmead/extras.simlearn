Design for entertainment

Posters, covers, thumbnails and title sequences decide what we watch and play. Pick a docket with your neighbour and talk through all four prompts.

Pick a docket with your neighbours and talk through its four questions. Explain your reasons, and ask what someone who disagrees would say.

All eight dockets

Docket 1

  1. Have you ever watched a film or series mainly because its poster or thumbnail looked good? Was it worth it?
  2. Why do so many posters in one genre look almost the same? Is that lazy design, or helpful for audiences?
  3. Should a poster explain the basic story or keep as much mystery as possible? Which gets more people into the cinema?
  4. Streaming services show different thumbnails of the same film to different users. Is that clever personalisation or manipulation?

Docket 2

  1. Which album cover, game cover or poster would you hang on your wall? Why that one?
  2. Book covers are often redesigned to match the film. Do you prefer the original cover or the film version? What does each lose?
  3. Is a poster's main job to represent the film honestly, or simply to sell tickets? Defend your choice.
  4. Trailers sometimes show scenes that aren't in the final film. Is that acceptable marketing, or cheating the audience?

Docket 3

  1. How do you choose what to watch on a streaming platform? How much does the image decide for you?
  2. Most games are now downloaded, not bought in boxes. Does a game still need cover art? What would be lost without it?
  3. Would you rather design the poster for a huge blockbuster seen by millions, or for a small film with complete creative freedom?
  4. Should age ratings and warnings appear clearly on posters and thumbnails, even if they spoil the design? Who should decide?

Docket 4

  1. Which film or game has a visual world you'd recognise from a single frame? What creates that look?
  2. Fan-made posters are sometimes better than the official ones. Why might a studio still choose the 'worse' design?
  3. A Berlin cinema wants a new identity: retro and cosy, or sleek and modern. Which would bring in more young people, and why?
  4. Should AI-generated art be allowed on official film posters? What would an illustrator say, and how would the studio reply?

Docket 5

  1. Have you ever felt tricked by a poster or trailer because the film was nothing like you expected? What happened?
  2. Why does so much entertainment rely on nostalgia: remakes, reboots, retro logos? What does that say about audiences?
  3. Your client wants a poster that will go viral online, even if it looks bad in a cinema foyer. Would you agree? Explain.
  4. Should theme parks and games be designed to make people spend as much as possible? Where does good design end and exploitation begin?

Docket 6

  1. Which video game menu or interface do you think is beautifully designed? What makes it work?
  2. Merchandise like T-shirts and toys often earns more than the film itself. Does that change what entertainment designers should focus on?
  3. Would you rather design the opening title sequence of a series or its poster? Which gives a designer more power?
  4. Horror posters on public billboards sometimes frighten children. Should there be stricter rules on where entertainment images can appear?

Docket 7

  1. What was your favourite cartoon or game as a child? What do you remember about how it looked?
  2. Is successful entertainment design the kind that wins awards, or the kind that sells the most tickets? Can it be both?
  3. A streaming service wants your design to work as a tiny phone thumbnail and a huge billboard. One design or two? What do you lose?
  4. Entertainment shapes how children imagine heroes, villains and beauty. How much responsibility do the designers have?

Docket 8

  1. Do you watch the opening titles of a series, or always press 'Skip intro'? What would make you watch?
  2. When a poster becomes iconic, designers copy it for decades. Is that homage, laziness or theft? Think of one you know.
  3. A small Edinburgh theatre festival needs a poster: a striking illustration, or a photo of the real venue and crowd? Which would you design, and why?
  4. Should entertainment designers avoid stereotypes, even when audiences find them funny and familiar? Who decides what counts as a stereotype?
