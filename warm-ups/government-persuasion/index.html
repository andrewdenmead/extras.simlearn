Government persuasion

Governments don't only make laws: they also try to change how we behave. Pick a docket, talk it through with your neighbour, and give reasons for your views.

Pick a docket with your neighbours and talk through its four questions. Explain your reasons, and ask what someone who disagrees would say.

All eight dockets

Docket 1

  1. Which public information campaign do you remember from childhood, about road safety, smoking or hand-washing? Why did it stick?
  2. Germany puts shocking pictures on cigarette packets. Is that fair persuasion or emotional manipulation? Where's the line?
  3. A campaign that frightens people into safer behaviour, or one that makes them feel guilty: which is more acceptable, and why?
  4. Should a government try to change what people do, or only inform them and let them choose? What would the other side say?

Docket 2

  1. Have you ever told a white lie to protect someone? Where do you draw the line between kindness and deception?
  2. Governments sometimes pay influencers to promote voting, vaccines or saving energy. How do you feel about that, and why?
  3. Which is more manipulative: an advert for a product, or a government campaign for a cause? Why?
  4. Is 'nudging' people, for example by making healthy food easier to pick, respectful or patronising? What would someone who disagrees say?

Docket 3

  1. What's the most persuasive advert you can remember? What did it do to you?
  2. In 2020 the German government made comic videos about 'heroes' who saved the country by staying on the sofa. Why do governments use humour?
  3. The same message from a ministry, or from celebrities who believe in it: which would you trust more, and why?
  4. Who should decide which behaviours a government campaigns for: ministers, scientists, Parliament or the public? What would the others say?

Docket 4

  1. Has a campaign ever made you change a habit, like recycling, wearing a helmet or saving energy? What made the difference?
  2. Why do people often ignore official warnings, like 'don't drink and drive' or 'wear sun cream'?
  3. To cut littering in Berlin, would you choose bigger fines or a clever campaign that makes litterers feel ashamed? Why?
  4. Is there a real difference between persuasion and propaganda, or is it just whether you agree with the message? What would someone who disagrees say?

Docket 5

  1. Do you trust official information from the government more or less than what your friends share online? Why?
  2. Why do you think trust in governments and the media has fallen in so many countries?
  3. As health minister, would you spend a campaign budget on TV adverts or on paying local doctors to talk to patients? Why?
  4. Should governments advertise at all, or should that money go straight into public services? Who would disagree?

Docket 6

  1. Think of a time someone persuaded you to do something good for you that you didn't want to do. How did they manage it?
  2. Parents bend the truth with small children, about Santa or the ice-cream van's music. When do children deserve the whole truth?
  3. Would you rather hear the full, complicated truth about a risk, or a simple message that gets you to act? Why?
  4. Should public broadcasters like ARD or the BBC help governments spread safety messages, or keep their distance? What's at stake?

Docket 7

  1. Which apps or websites are best at getting you to do things you didn't plan to do? How do they do it?
  2. Is it worse when a company or when a government uses psychological tricks on you? Why?
  3. A BVG poster telling passengers what to do, or one showing what most passengers already do: which would work better, and why?
  4. When a public campaign backfires, who should be held responsible: the ministry, the agency that made it, or nobody? Why?

Docket 8

  1. Which source would you trust most for a health warning: a doctor, a ministry, a newspaper or a friend? Why?
  2. Do people resent being told what's good for them more than they fear the risk itself? Why?
  3. Ban something dangerous, or persuade people to avoid it: which is more respectful of citizens, and which works better?
  4. How much should a government try to change citizens' behaviour, rather than just making good laws? What are the risks either way?
