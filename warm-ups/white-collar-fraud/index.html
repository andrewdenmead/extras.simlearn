Warm-up · EIM · C1

White-collar fraud

Fraud in offices and boardrooms rarely looks like a crime at first. Why do people do it, and why do others go along? Pick a docket with your neighbour and talk through all four prompts.

Pick a docket with your neighbours and talk through its four questions. Explain your reasons, and ask what someone who disagrees would say.

All eight dockets

Docket 1

  1. Where's the line between 'selling yourself' on a CV and lying? Give an example of each.
  2. Why do you think intelligent, well-paid people commit fraud? Is it greed, pressure, or something else?
  3. Would you rather work for a boss who is brilliant but bends the rules, or one who follows every rule but is mediocre? Why?
  4. Wirecard was one of Germany's most celebrated tech companies before its fraud came out. Why do people want to believe in success stories so much?

Docket 2

  1. Think of small 'everyday frauds' people commit without feeling guilty: fare dodging, fake sick days, inflated expense claims. Why don't they feel guilty?
  2. Are sales targets a useful motivator, or do they push people towards dishonesty? What would a sales manager say?
  3. Would you rather be known as completely honest but a bit naïve, or clever and flexible with the truth? Which helps more in business?
  4. Someone who steals a phone may face tougher consequences than a manager who misleads investors out of millions. Why might that be, and what would be fairer?

Docket 3

  1. Have you been in a group project or job where everyone knew something wasn't right but nobody said anything? Why did people stay quiet?
  2. How much do people fake success online, with holidays, jobs or lifestyles? Is a little faking just normal now?
  3. Would you rather join a fast-growing start-up where the rules are loose, or an established firm where everything needs approval? What risks come with each?
  4. Fraud often begins with one small 'temporary' fix that grows. Why is it so hard to stop once it has started?

Docket 4

  1. What's the most convincing scam message, email, text or call, that you or someone you know has received? What made it believable?
  2. Why do companies sometimes reward employees who hit their targets without asking how they did it?
  3. Would you rather your company had strict rules that slow everything down, or trusted staff and accepted occasional abuse? Defend your choice.
  4. Some fraudsters become famous, with films and series made about them. Why are we fascinated by them, and is that a problem?

Docket 5

  1. Have you ever bought something that turned out to be very different from what was promised? How did the seller explain it?
  2. In some industries a dubious practice is so common that refusing it loses you customers. As a new manager, would you refuse or adapt? What would that cost you?
  3. Would you rather work for a company that pays big bonuses for results, or steady salaries with no bonuses? Which encourages more honest behaviour?
  4. Should bosses be personally liable, with their own money or freedom, for fraud in their company even if they didn't plan it? What would a CEO say?

Docket 6

  1. When you hear 'white-collar crime', what kind of people and situations do you imagine? Where does that image come from?
  2. How far is fraud caused by a company's culture rather than a few 'bad apples'? What would someone who disagrees say?
  3. Would you rather earn a lot at a company you suspect cuts corners, or less at one you're sure is clean? Be honest!
  4. Influencers sometimes promote investments they don't understand. When followers lose money, how much is the influencer to blame, and how much the followers?

Docket 7

  1. Have you ever noticed an advert or offer that turned out to be clearly misleading? How did you find out?
  2. Why do you think so many fraud cases involve start-ups and fast-growing tech companies?
  3. Would you rather your university used AI to catch cheating in every exam, or trusted students and caught fewer cheaters? Who wins and who loses?
  4. Big frauds often hurt employees and small investors most, not the people who planned them. Who should pay for the damage, and how?

Docket 8

  1. What's a grey area at work or university you're never quite sure about, like using AI, sharing notes or rounding up hours? Where do you draw the line?
  2. Why do respected people, such as professors or chief executives, sometimes find it easier to get away with dishonesty?
  3. Would you prefer laws that punish fraud very harshly, or rules that make it very hard to commit in the first place? Which works better?
  4. Investors usually want companies to grow fast. How far does that pressure share the blame when founders cheat? What would an investor say?
