Writing exam: how to get a good mark

English for Business Communication · C1

1The task

The writing part is one quarter of your exam (with listening, reading and speaking), and it gets one overall grade. You have about 45 minutes to make two A/B decisions and write a recommendation memo of about 250 words to two named people at the company.

  • The decisions: two questions, each with option A or B. There is no single right combination. Your grade is for how well you argue, not for which options you pick: any choice you state clearly and justify reasonably gets credit.
  • Your readers: two people from the story, named in the task. They know their company well and have their own worries. They need a clear decision and good reasons, not a summary of the paper.
  • Your material: everything else in the paper (the three recordings, the article and the two emails). The facts, numbers and opinions you need are all in there.
  • The instructions always ask you to: make a clear recommendation on both decisions and explain how they affect each other; use information from the recordings, the article and the emails, including the numbers; answer the strongest objection to your plan; and write for your two readers, keeping their concerns in mind.

Aim for about 250 words. The word count itself isn't marked, but much shorter usually means a part of the task is thin, and much longer usually means you are summarising.

2How it is marked

Your memo gets one overall grade, judged as university English at C1 (the course level). The examiner reads the whole text and asks how well it does the task. Nothing is added up.

  • The five criteria are labels, not points. Task and reader, Using the information, Building the argument, Organisation and flow and Language (below) each get Exam-ready, Nearly there or Not yet. They show you what to work on next. The grade is one judgement of the whole text, so a Nearly there or two can still be in the 1 range.
  • It's a language exam, so your choice isn't marked. There is no correct combination of options, and you aren't graded on business, design or subject knowledge. What counts is how well you communicate and argue: in English class, two plus two is five is good English; to and too be foar is bad English. So you get credit when you state your choice clearly and make a reasonable attempt to justify it from the paper. You don't need the best possible source, argument or counterargument.
  • Report the paper accurately. Getting a figure, a date or what someone said wrong is a weakness, because reading and reporting are part of the task. So is a reason that doesn't follow from what you cite.
  • The word count isn't marked. About 250 words is simply the length that lets you cover the task. A shorter text is only weaker if it leaves out something the task asks for.
  • At C1, the English should be clear, precise and mostly accurate, in a professional style. Errors that don't get in the way of your reader are normal at this level.
  • No deduction for every slip. One missing detail or a few language errors don't each cost you something: what counts is how much the weaknesses limit the text as a whole.

A clear, competent memo is in the 1 range. It doesn't have to be perfect: clear and well organised, with your sources reported accurately and only small slips, is a 1,0 or 1,3; with some noticeable language errors, about a 1,3 or 1,7. In about 250 words you can't include everything: a few well-chosen facts, with their sources, and the main objection answered in one or two sentences are enough. These are the same five criteria and labels as in the feedback you get after every simulation, and its tips follow this guide, so every sim is practice for the exam.

Task and readera label, not points

Give a clear answer to the brief, written for your reader.

Exam-readyThe recommendation is clear from the start and every part of the brief is covered in proportion. The text is written for the named reader throughout: the right format, level of detail and tone.
Not yetThere is a recommendation, but it comes late or is hedged, or one part of the brief is missing. The text reads more like a summary of the sources than a document for this reader. At its weakest: no clear recommendation, or most of the brief is not addressed; the reader and purpose are unclear.
Using the informationa label, not points

Choose, combine and report the information that decides the question.

Exam-readyUses the decisive information from across the sources (in a sim, the group's), accurately and in the writer's own words, and combines sources to make points none of them makes alone. Key facts are credited (who said it) and linked to what they mean for the decision.
Not yetUses some relevant information, but relies on one or two sources, lists facts rather than using them, or copies wording closely; a key fact is missing or slightly wrong. At its weakest: little information from the simulation, or it is inaccurate, uncredited or irrelevant to the decision.
Building the argumenta label, not points

Make a convincing case: reasons, evidence and consequences, with the other side answered.

Exam-readyA convincing, well-prioritised case: each main point is supported by evidence and its consequences are spelled out; the strongest counter-argument or trade-off is acknowledged and answered; the remaining risk is named with a way to manage it.
Not yetRelevant points, but often asserted rather than supported, or all treated as equally important; the other side is ignored or only mentioned. At its weakest: little reasoning: opinions or a list of choices without support, or points that contradict each other.
Organisation and flowa label, not points

Guide your reader through the text.

Exam-readyLogically structured for its purpose; each paragraph develops one idea; varied, natural linking shows how ideas relate, so the reader never has to work out the connection.
Not yetA recognisable structure, but paragraphs mix ideas or the order makes the reader work; linking is limited or sometimes misused. At its weakest: little structure: a block of text or a list of disconnected points.
Languagea label, not points

Say exactly what you mean, accurately and professionally.

Exam-readyA range that lets the writer say exactly what they mean, used precisely and naturally for the course's target level; errors are rare and minor; the style is consistently professional.
Not yetEnough range for the main points, but sometimes imprecise or repetitive; noticeable errors that occasionally make the reader pause; some slips in style. At its weakest: a limited range for the task; frequent errors that make parts hard to follow.

Three criteria are about thinking (task, information, argument), so planning matters as much as language.

3A structure that works

Put your recommendation first, give one paragraph to each decision, then face the objection. This order covers every part of the brief and does most of the work for Organisation and flow by itself.

This is a guide, not a template. If your memo is close to it (two paragraphs merged, a part a little longer or shorter, the objection answered inside a decision paragraph), you lose nothing, as long as your readers can follow it and every part of the task is there.

1. Recommendationabout 40 words
Both decisions in one or two sentences, plus the main reason. Your readers should know what you want after reading this alone.
2. Decision 1about 60 words
Topic sentence with your choice. Then two pieces of evidence, each credited (As Kirsty points out…), with numbers, and what they mean for the company.
3. Decision 2about 60 words
The same pattern, and how this decision affects the first one (Opening in X also means that…).
4. Objection and riskabout 60 words
The strongest argument against your plan, said fairly and with its source, then your answer. Name the risk that remains and how to manage it.
5. Next stepsabout 30 words
One or two concrete actions: who does what, by when, or what to check first.

A few tips:

  • Skip the memo header. No To, From or Subject lines: what is marked is the body of your text, so spend your time there.
  • Headings are optional. Short paragraph headings (Recommendation, Risks) are fine in a memo. Bullet points are fine for next steps, but not for the argument itself.
  • Write to your readers. Use you and we. Mention their concerns by name (Bastian, I know you are worried about…).
  • Don't retell the story. Your readers know their own company. Every sentence should help them decide.

4Using your 45 minutes

Spend about ten minutes planning before you write a sentence. A planned 250 words beats an unplanned 350 on almost every criterion.

  1. Before the writing part. The situation at the start names the two questions. While you do the listening and reading, mark facts, numbers and opinions that bear on them, and note who said each one.
  2. Minutes 0–10: decide and plan. For each decision, jot down the two or three strongest facts for A and for B, with their sources. Choose. Write down how the two decisions affect each other, and the strongest objection to your plan.
  3. Minutes 10–35: write. Follow the structure above. Write the recommendation paragraph first. If you run short of time, it is the part that matters most.
  4. Minutes 35–45: check. Go through the checklist at the end of this guide, fix grammar and spelling, and check the word count.

5A model memo

This example is invented and much simpler than a real paper, but it shows the shape. The other options could be argued just as well: the grade comes from how the case is made, not from the choice.

The situation: Fernhill Coffee, a small roaster in Leeds with three cafés and an online shop, has an offer from the supermarket chain Northway. Decision 1: A, accept the offer; B, turn it down and grow online. Decision 2: A, keep the £9 price per bag; B, launch a cheaper everyday range at £6. The readers are Kirsty Kinloch (founder) and Bastian Brinkmann (finance director). The "paper" says that online sales earned £84,000 last year at £3.50 a bag, but grew only 8% (Bastian, in the meeting); that Kirsty fears a supermarket deal would cheapen the brand (meeting); that Northway expects 40,000 bags in year one at £1.20 a bag for Fernhill, and wants a shelf price of about £6; that own-label coffee sales rose 15% last year (article); that Northway can end the deal after twelve months (its offer); and that café regulars worry about the brand (email from the café manager).

We recommend accepting Northway's offer (Decision 1, A) and selling only a new, cheaper everyday range there (Decision 2, B). Together, they bring in new customers without making the Fernhill name cheaper.

Supermarket shelves would reach far more people than our shop can. Northway forecasts 40,000 bags in the first year, which at £1.20 a bag means about £48,000. Online growth, by contrast, slowed to 8% last year, as Bastian pointed out in the meeting, which would add only about £7,000.

The two decisions depend on each other. Northway needs a price of around £6, and the article shows why: shoppers are switching to cheaper coffee, and own-label sales rose 15% last year. Our £9 bags would struggle on that shelf. An everyday range at £6 meets Northway's price, while the premium beans stay in our cafés and online, where they earn £3.50 a bag.

The strongest objection is Kirsty's: a supermarket deal could make Fernhill look like any other brand, and the café manager reports that regulars already fear this. That is why the everyday range should have its own name and packaging. The risk that remains is that Northway can drop us after twelve months, so we should keep investing in online subscriptions rather than relying on one buyer.

As a next step, we suggest agreeing a six-month review with Northway and testing two names for the everyday range with our regulars before we sign.

237 words. Why it would get a grade in the 1 range:

Task and reader
Both decisions in the first sentence. It speaks to Kirsty's worry and uses Bastian's figure.
Using the information
Numbers from the meeting, the article and the email, each credited, and each followed by what it means (which would add only about £7,000). The article and Northway's price are combined to explain the link between the decisions.
Building the argument
Point, evidence, so what in each paragraph. The objection is put at its strongest and answered with a concrete change (own name and packaging). The remaining risk has a way to manage it.
Organisation and flow
One idea per paragraph, each opened by a topic sentence. Linking shows the logic: by contrast, and the article shows why, That is why, so.
Language
Precise business vocabulary (forecast, everyday range, relying on one buyer). Claims are careful (would struggle, could make). Professional, but direct.

6Useful language and common mistakes

RecommendWe recommend…On balance, the stronger option is…I would advise against…
Report a sourceAs Bastian points out, …According to the article, …In her email, X warns that…
Say what a fact meansThis means that…which would add only…As a result, …
Link the decisionsThe two decisions depend on each other.If we choose A, then…This also affects…
Set conditionsprovided that…as long as…only if…
Face the objectionThe strongest argument against this is…This is a fair concern, but…To reduce this risk, we should…
Be careful with claimsis likely tocouldwould probablysuggests that

The mistakes that hold a text back most:

  • Summarising instead of recommending. Retelling the recordings in order, then giving an opinion at the end.
  • Two separate answers. Treating the decisions as if they had nothing to do with each other.
  • Numbers without meaning. "Sales rose 15%." So what? Say what it means for this company.
  • Only one source. Using just the emails and forgetting the recordings and the article.
  • Mentioning the objection without answering it. "Some people may disagree" is not an answer.
  • Copying sentences from the paper. Report in your own words, and say who said it.
  • Chatty style. Honestly, a lot of, super important, exclamation marks.
