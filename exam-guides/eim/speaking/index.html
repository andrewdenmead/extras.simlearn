Speaking exam: how to get a good mark

English for International Management · C1

The examHow it is markedUsing your 20 minutesYour openingDuring the negotiationThe examiner's questionsUseful languageCommon mistakesFinal checklist

1The exam

The speaking exam is worth 25 of your 100 points. It is a negotiation in a group of two, three or four. You each represent one party (a company, a person or an organisation), and together you must agree on one deal.

  • You are a representative, not the party itself: "You represent…". You speak for your client and pass on what your client and your contacts have told you.
  • Preparation, about 20 minutes: you read the situation, your brief, three emails and the deal sheet, on the exam page. Some of your contacts can each tell you one more thing: you choose one of three pieces of information from each, and you can't change your choice, so read the options carefully.
  • In the room, about 10 minutes per student:
    1. Your opening (about one minute each): who you represent, what they want and the most important thing you have found out.
    2. The negotiation: you agree on A or B for each point on the deal sheet. You can add conditions to an option, as long as everyone agrees. During the negotiation, the examiner reads out a piece of breaking news that changes the situation.
    3. Questions from the examiner about the deal you made.
  • Notes: you can bring short notes (key words and numbers), not full sentences. Pass on what you read in your own words: don't read it out.
  • Your mark is your own. Each of you is marked separately. The deal itself isn't marked: there is no "right" deal. What counts is how you use your information and how you negotiate.

2How it is marked

The examiner marks you live on five criteria, each out of 5 points, judged at C1 level. You don't need to speak above that level. Grades use the usual scale: 95 per cent of the points for a 1,0, 90 per cent for a 1,3, and so on in steps of five down to 50 per cent for a 4,0. For the 25 speaking points, that means:

Range5 points

Enough vocabulary and structures to say what you mean in this situation, including the language of negotiating.

Full marks (5)A broad range of vocabulary and structures for the topic and for negotiating (proposing, conceding, conditions), used flexibly; rarely has to search for or avoid what they want to say.
2–3 pointsEnough language for simple points and positions, but limited for nuance or negotiating: repetition, vague words, some ideas left unsaid.
Accuracy5 points

Grammar and word choice that don't get in the way of understanding.

Full marks (5)Consistently good control at the level; errors are rare, minor and often self-corrected.
2–3 pointsReasonable control of simple structures; frequent errors in more complex language, sometimes making the meaning unclear.
Fluency5 points

Speaking at length and keeping going, with pauses only to think about what to say.

Full marks (5)Speaks at length with an easy, natural flow; pauses are for content, not language. The opening statement is clear and well organised.
2–3 pointsCan produce short stretches, but hesitation and false starts are noticeable; longer turns lose their thread.
Interaction5 points

Listening and responding to the others, and moving the group towards an agreement.

Full marks (5)Responds to and builds on others' points, asks good questions, proposes and tests compromises, and helps the group move forward; takes and gives turns naturally.
2–3 pointsTakes part, but mainly states their own position; limited response to what others say.
Mediation5 points

Passing on what you read and heard, clearly and accurately, in your own words, so the others can use it.

Full marks (5)Relays the key information from their documents and recordings accurately and selectively, in their own words, and explains what it means for the deal; checks that others have understood.
2–3 pointsPasses on some information, but incompletely, inaccurately or mostly by reading aloud; relevance is left unclear.

Interaction and mediation are 10 of your 25 points. Talking a lot isn't enough: what counts is how you listen, pass on information and move the group towards a deal.

3Using your 20 minutes

  1. Minutes 0–5: your brief. Who is your client? What do they want, and what matters most to them? What does the brief tell you to find out?
  2. Minutes 5–12: the emails. Mark the facts and numbers your client and your contacts give you, and the ones the others at the table need to hear. Note who said what.
  3. Minute 12: one more thing. For each contact who offers one, choose the piece of information that is most useful for the deal. The best ones often open a middle way, or give you something another party needs.
  4. Minutes 12–20: plan. For each point on the deal sheet, decide what your client would like, what they could accept, and a condition you could offer. Note the key words and numbers for your opening, but not full sentences.

4Your opening

About one minute, three parts: who you represent, what they want and the most important thing you have found out. Finish by saying where you would like to start. An invented example (100 words, about a minute at a calm pace), for the works council of a customer-service centre:

Good morning. I represent the works council at the Leipzig customer-service centre, about 300 staff. My client accepts that the new owners want to bring in AI for routine enquiries, but it wants a guarantee that nobody is made redundant in the first two years. The most important thing I've found out is that around 60 staff are due to retire or leave in the next three years anyway, so most of the change can happen through natural turnover. That means a no-redundancy guarantee may cost the owners much less than they expect. I'd like to start with the timetable.

Why it works: it is short and organised, it gives one key fact with a number, it says what the fact means for the deal (mediation), and it invites the others in (interaction).

5During the negotiation

  • Start with questions. Each of you knows things the others don't. Ask what they found out before you argue: What does your client need here?
  • Pass on information, then say what it means. My client's contact told us that… so option B would cost them…
  • Propose with conditions. We could accept A, as long as… · What if we agree B, but with…?
  • Look for middle ways. If two of you want different options, a condition can often give both something. Your "one more thing" may help here.
  • Bring others in. If someone is quiet, ask them: Deniz, what does this mean for your client? That is interaction, and it often moves the deal on.
  • Summarise. Every few minutes: So far we agree on… We still need to decide…
  • When the breaking news comes, stop and think together: which point does it change, and for whom? Be ready to reopen a decision you have already made.
  • Don't agree too fast. Use your information before you give way, and say what you want in return.

6The examiner's questions

At the end, the examiner asks about the deal: why you chose it, what your client gave up, what risk is left. Answer with reasons and your information, not just "it was fair". It's fine to say what you are less happy with: My client gave way on…, because… The risk is…

8Common mistakes

  • Reading out the emails. Pass the information on in your own words, with the key numbers.
  • A memorised speech. Prepare key words, not sentences. A learned text sounds learned and leaves you stuck when the talk changes direction.
  • Only stating your position. Repeating what your client wants without listening or responding costs interaction marks.
  • Keeping your information to yourself. It's only useful if the others hear it and understand what it means.
  • Talking over others, or waiting to be asked. Take turns: speak, then give the floor back.
  • Agreeing to everything at once. A quick deal leaves you little to say and no information used.
  • Ignoring the breaking news. It is there to change the deal: show how it affects your client.
