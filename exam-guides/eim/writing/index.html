Writing exam: how to get a good mark

English for International Management · C1

The taskHow it is markedA structure that worksUsing your 45 minutesA model memoUseful language and common mistakesFinal checklist

1The task

The writing part is worth 25 of your 100 exam points (the written paper is 75, the speaking exam 25). You have about 45 minutes to make two A/B management decisions and write a recommendation memo of about 250 words to two named managers at the company.

  • The decisions: two questions, each with option A or B. There is no single right combination. You get marks for how well you argue, not for which options you pick.
  • Your readers: two managers from the story, named in the task. They know their company and have different priorities, often money against operations or people. They need a clear decision they can act on, not a summary of the paper.
  • Your material: everything else in the paper (the three recordings, the article and the two emails). The facts, numbers and opinions you need are all in there.
  • The instructions always ask you to: make a clear recommendation on both decisions and explain how they affect each other; use information from the recordings, the article and the emails, including the numbers; answer the strongest objection to your plan; and write for your two readers, keeping their concerns in mind.

Aim for about 250 words. Much shorter usually means a part of the task is thin. Much longer usually means you are summarising.

2How it is marked

The memo is marked on five criteria, each out of 5 points, judged at C1 level. You don't need to write above that level. Grades use the usual scale: 95 per cent of the points for a 1,0, 90 per cent for a 1,3, and so on in steps of five down to 50 per cent for a 4,0. For the 25 writing points, that means:

These are the same five criteria as in the feedback you get after every simulation. It rates each one Exam-ready (full marks), Nearly there (4) or Not yet (3 or less), and its tips follow this guide, so every sim is practice for the exam.

Task and reader5 points

Give a clear answer to the brief, written for your reader.

Full marks (5)The recommendation is clear from the start and every part of the brief is covered in proportion. The text is written for the named reader throughout: the right format, level of detail and tone.
2–3 pointsThere is a recommendation, but it comes late or is hedged, or one part of the brief is missing. The text reads more like a summary of the sources than a document for this reader.
Using the information5 points

Choose, combine and report the information that decides the question.

Full marks (5)Uses the decisive information from across the sources (in a sim, the group's), accurately and in the writer's own words, and combines sources to make points none of them makes alone. Every key fact is credited and linked to what it means for the decision.
2–3 pointsUses some relevant information, but relies on one or two sources, lists facts rather than using them, or copies wording closely; a key fact is missing or slightly wrong.
Building the argument5 points

Make a convincing case: reasons, evidence and consequences, with the other side answered.

Full marks (5)A convincing, well-prioritised case: each main point is supported by evidence and its consequences are spelled out; the strongest counter-argument or trade-off is acknowledged and answered; the remaining risk is named with a way to manage it.
2–3 pointsRelevant points, but often asserted rather than supported, or all treated as equally important; the other side is ignored or only mentioned.
Organisation and flow5 points

Guide your reader through the text.

Full marks (5)Logically structured for its purpose; each paragraph develops one idea; varied, natural linking shows how ideas relate, so the reader never has to work out the connection.
2–3 pointsA recognisable structure, but paragraphs mix ideas or the order makes the reader work; linking is limited or sometimes misused.
Language5 points

Say exactly what you mean, accurately and professionally.

Full marks (5)A range that lets the writer say exactly what they mean, used precisely and naturally for the course's target level; errors are rare and minor; the style is consistently professional.
2–3 pointsEnough range for the main points, but sometimes imprecise or repetitive; noticeable errors that occasionally make the reader pause; some slips in style.

Three criteria are about thinking (task, information, argument), so planning matters as much as language.

3A structure that works

Put your recommendation first, give one paragraph to each decision, then face the objection. This order covers every part of the brief and earns most of the organisation marks by itself.

1. Recommendationabout 40 words
Both decisions in one or two sentences, plus the main reason. Your readers should know what you want after reading this alone.
2. Decision 1about 60 words
Topic sentence with your choice. Then two pieces of evidence, each credited (As the finance director points out…), with numbers, and what they mean for the company.
3. Decision 2about 60 words
The same pattern, and how this decision affects the first one (Commission works best for people who…, which is why…).
4. Objection and riskabout 60 words
The strongest argument against your plan, said fairly and with its source, then your answer. Name the risk that remains and how to manage it.
5. Next stepsabout 30 words
One or two concrete actions: who does what, by when, or what to check first.

A few tips:

  • Skip the memo header. No To, From or Subject lines: what is marked is the body of your text, so spend your time there.
  • Headings are optional. Short paragraph headings (Recommendation, Risks) are fine in a memo. Bullet points are fine for next steps, but not for the argument itself.
  • Write to your readers. Use you and we, and speak to each manager's priority (Matthias, I know the cost worries you…). When the two readers disagree, show you have heard both.
  • Do the maths for them. Managers decide on numbers: turn the figures in the paper into costs, savings, payback times or risks.
  • Don't retell the story. Your readers know their own company. Every sentence should help them decide.

4Using your 45 minutes

Spend about ten minutes planning before you write a sentence. A planned 250 words beats an unplanned 350 on almost every criterion.

  1. Before the writing part. The situation at the start names the two questions. While you do the listening and reading, mark facts, numbers and opinions that bear on them, and note who said each one.
  2. Minutes 0–10: decide and plan. For each decision, jot down the two or three strongest facts for A and for B, with their sources. Do any sums you need. Choose. Write down how the two decisions affect each other, and the strongest objection to your plan.
  3. Minutes 10–35: write. Follow the structure above. Write the recommendation paragraph first. If you run short of time, it is the part that matters most.
  4. Minutes 35–45: check. Go through the checklist at the end of this guide, fix grammar and spelling, and check the word count.

5A model memo

This example is invented and much simpler than a real paper, but it shows the shape.

The situation: Brakel Pumps, a Dutch maker of industrial pumps with 25 sellers, wants to grow its sales. Decision 1: A, pay the sales team partly on commission; B, keep fixed salaries. Decision 2: A, fill five open sales posts through a new training academy for graduates; B, hire experienced sellers. The readers are Ottilie Bellini (CEO) and Matthias Roeder (finance director). The "paper" says that sales have been flat at about €40 million for three years (radio report); that Sjoerd, head of sales, thinks experienced sellers expect commission and trainees would earn little at first, and Matthias fears costs will rise (meeting); that firms in the industry that pay commission sell about 12% more, but lose 30% of their staff a year when pay is mainly commission (article); and that the academy would cost €400,000 a year and take 18 months to produce its first sellers, while five experienced sellers would cost about €100,000 a year more than graduates (email from Ximena, HR).

I recommend paying our sales team partly on commission (Decision 1, A) and filling the five open posts with experienced sellers rather than through a training academy (Decision 2, B).

Our sales have been flat at about €40 million for three years, as the radio report points out, while competitors are growing. According to the article, firms in our industry that pay commission sell about 12% more. A mix of 70% fixed salary and 30% commission would reward the sellers who bring in new customers without putting everyone's income at risk.

The two decisions depend on each other. Commission works best for people who can sell from day one. Sjoerd made this point in the meeting: experienced sellers expect commission, while trainees would earn very little in their first year and might leave. Ximena's email adds that the academy would take 18 months to produce its first sellers, which is too slow if we want growth now.

Matthias's objection is the strongest one: commission and experienced staff will raise our costs. However, the five experienced sellers would cost about €100,000 a year more than new graduates, while the academy would cost €400,000 a year, and commission is only paid on sales we actually make. The remaining risk is staff turnover, which the article puts at 30% in firms that pay mainly by commission. Keeping most of the salary fixed should limit this, and we should review it after a year.

As next steps, I suggest that Ximena advertises the five posts this month and that Matthias models the 70/30 pay scheme.

261 words. Why it would score well:

Task and reader
Both decisions in the first sentence. It answers each reader: the costs for Matthias, a clear plan for growth for Ottilie.
Using the information
Figures and views from the radio report, the meeting, the article and the email, each credited. The writer compares the costs (€100,000 against €400,000 a year) instead of just repeating numbers. Sjoerd's view and Ximena's email are combined to link the two decisions.
Building the argument
Point, evidence, so what in each paragraph. Matthias's objection is put fairly and answered with figures, and the remaining risk (turnover) has a way to manage it: a middle way of 70% fixed pay and a review after a year.
Organisation and flow
One idea per paragraph, each opened by a topic sentence. Linking shows the logic: while, The two decisions depend on each other, adds that, However.
Language
Precise management vocabulary (commission, fixed salary, staff turnover, models the pay scheme). Claims are careful (might leave, should limit). Professional, but direct.

6Useful language and common mistakes

Report a sourceAs Matthias points out, …According to the article, …In her email, Ximena warns that…
Work with numbersThis costs us about… a year…while… would cost…This outweighs…
Link the decisionsThe two decisions belong together.If we choose A, then…This also affects…
Set conditionsprovided that…as long as…only if…
Face the objectionThe strongest argument against this is…This is a fair concern, but…To limit this risk, we could…
Be careful with claimsis likely tocouldmightsuggests that

The mistakes that cost the most marks:

  • Summarising instead of recommending. Retelling the recordings in order, then giving an opinion at the end.
  • Two separate answers. Treating the decisions as if they had nothing to do with each other.
  • Numbers without meaning. "Sales are flat at €40 million." So what? Say what it means for the decision.
  • Only one source. Using just the emails and forgetting the recordings and the article.
  • Taking one manager's side and ignoring the other. Your two readers often want different things: show you have heard both.
  • Mentioning the objection without answering it. "Some people may disagree" is not an answer.
  • Copying sentences from the paper, or chatty style. Report in your own words, and keep it professional: no honestly, a lot of, super important or exclamation marks.
