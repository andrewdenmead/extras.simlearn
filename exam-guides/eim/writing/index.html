Writing exam: how to get a good mark

English for International Management · C1

1The task

The writing part is one quarter of your exam (with listening, reading and speaking), and it gets one overall grade. You have about 45 minutes to make two A/B management decisions and write a recommendation memo of about 250 words to two named managers at the company.

  • The decisions: two questions, each with option A or B. There is no single right combination. Your grade is for how well you argue, not for which options you pick: any choice you state clearly and justify reasonably gets credit.
  • Your readers: two managers from the story, named in the task. They know their company and have different priorities, often money against operations or people. They need a clear decision they can act on, not a summary of the paper.
  • Your material: everything else in the paper (the three recordings, the article and the two emails). The facts, numbers and opinions you need are all in there.
  • The instructions always ask you to: make a clear recommendation on both decisions and explain how they affect each other; use information from the recordings, the article and the emails, including the numbers; answer the strongest objection to your plan; and write for your two readers, keeping their concerns in mind.

Aim for about 250 words. The word count itself isn't marked, but much shorter usually means a part of the task is thin, and much longer usually means you are summarising.

2How it is marked

Your memo gets one overall grade, judged as university English at C1 (the course level). The examiner reads the whole text and asks how well it does the task. Nothing is added up.

  • The grade comes from your argument moves (AMs). The examiner looks for eight moves (below): Position, Reasoning, Evidence, Counter-argument, Response and Conclusion, plus two extras, Source check and Trade-off. Each move reaches a level. For evidence and the other side: your own reasoning is good, a source from the paper is better, the best source in the paper is best. Your writing gets up to 25 points: the moves for each decision, plus the conclusion. All the main moves done well on both decisions is a 1,0; the two extras fill gaps elsewhere.
  • It's a language exam, so your choice isn't marked. There is no correct combination of options, and you aren't graded on business, design or subject knowledge. What counts is how well you communicate and argue: in English class, two plus two is five is good English; to and too be foar is bad English. So you get credit when you state your choice clearly and make a reasonable attempt to justify it from the paper. Either option can reach the top grade: the best source and the strongest objection exist for both sides.
  • Report the paper accurately. Getting a figure, a date or what someone said wrong is a weakness, because reading and reporting are part of the task. So is a reason that doesn't follow from what you cite.
  • The word count isn't marked. About 250 words is simply the length that lets you cover the task. A shorter text is only weaker if it leaves out something the task asks for.
  • At C1, the English should be clear, precise and mostly accurate, in a professional style. Errors that don't get in the way of your reader are normal at this level.
  • Language costs one step at most. Errors only lower the grade when they sometimes hide what you mean, and then by one step (for example 1,3 to 1,7). Small slips cost nothing.

How your moves become the grade. Your writing gets up to 25 points. For each of the two decisions: position (1), reasoning (2), evidence (3), counter-argument (3) and response (2), so 11 + 11; then the conclusion (3) for the whole text. A source check or a trade-off (up to 2 each) fills gaps, never above 25. 24-25 points = 1,0 · 21-23 = 1,3 · 18-20 = 1,7 · 16-17 = 2,0 · 14-15 = 2,3 · 13 = 2,7 · 11-12 = 3,0 · 9-10 = 3,3 · 7-8 = 3,7 · 5-6 = 4,0. So a clear text that uses sources from the paper on both decisions and answers a real objection is about 20 (1,7); add a trade-off and it is about 21 (1,3); the same text built only on your own reasoning is about 14 (2,3). Language errors cost one step at most, and only when they sometimes hide the meaning.

A clear, competent memo is in the 1 range. It doesn't have to be perfect: clear and well organised, with your sources reported accurately and only small slips, is a 1,0 or 1,3; with some noticeable language errors, about a 1,3 or 1,7. In about 250 words you can't include everything: a few well-chosen facts, with their sources, and the main objection answered in one or two sentences are enough. These are the same eight moves as in the feedback you get after every simulation (it shows your text rewritten, with what to add in colour, and your grade), and as in Write It Well (extras.simlearn.online/exam-writing/), so every sim is practice for the exam.

Position: What do you think?a main move

Say your choice clearly, near the start, so the reader knows what you want them to decide or do. e.g. Vossberg should keep at least one staffed till in every city store.

ClearThe choice is clear and comes early (in a whole report: a clear choice on each decision).
UnclearThere is a choice, but it comes late, is hedged until it means little, or doesn't say what to do.
MissingNo choice is stated, or it can't be found.
Reasoning: Why?a main move

Explain why your choice serves the goal: the logic that links it to what the reader cares about. e.g. City stores depend on regular older customers, who are the most likely to leave if a store feels impersonal.

Linked to the caseA reason tied to this case and its goal (these customers, this deadline, this risk).
GeneralA reason that would fit any case ('change is risky', 'it is modern'), or one that doesn't really follow.
MissingNo reason is given: the choice is only stated or repeated.
Evidence: What is it based on?a main move

Back your reason with facts or figures from the texts, and say who they come from. e.g. According to the finance director, basket value fell by 6% during the pilot.

The best sourceThe most decisive evidence the texts give for the writer's side (the key's 'best' evidence), reported accurately, with who said it.
A sourceA fact from the texts, reported accurately, but not the most decisive one available.
Own reasoningSupport from the writer's own ideas or general knowledge, not from the texts.
MissingNothing supports the choice.
Source check: How far can we trust it?an extra

Say in a clause how much weight a piece of evidence can carry: a small sample, the wrong season, a source with an interest, old data, one case only. e.g. That figure needs care, however, because the pilot ran over the summer.

SpecificA specific weakness of a specific source, taken from the texts (who has an interest, how small, how old, when).
GeneralA general doubt ('this may not be reliable') without saying why.
MissingEvidence is used without any comment on how far to trust it (fine: this is an extra).
Counter-argument: What does the other side say?a main move

Give the strongest point against your choice, fairly, and say who makes it. e.g. The operations manager argues that full self-checkout would save €2.1 million a year.

The strongest oneThe strongest objection the texts raise against the writer's side (the key's), stated fairly, with who makes it.
From a sourceA real objection from the texts, but not the strongest one against the writer's side.
Own ideaAn objection from the writer's own head, or a weak or generic one ('some people may disagree').
MissingThe other side doesn't appear.
Response: Why does your view still win?a main move

Answer the counter-argument: admit what is true in it, then show its limit with a reason or a fact. e.g. This is a real saving, but it assumes no extra losses from theft.

AnsweredThe true part is admitted and its limit shown with a reason or a fact from the texts.
AssertedAn answer, but only an opinion ('I still think…'), with no reason or fact.
MissingThe counter-argument is left unanswered, dismissed ('they are wrong') or attacks the person.
Trade-off: What does it cost?an extra

Name what your choice gives up, and why that price is worth paying. e.g. Keeping one staffed till means giving up a third of the savings, but that protects our most frequent customers.

Named and justifiedThe cost is named and the writer says why it is worth paying or how to limit it.
MentionedA cost or risk is mentioned, but not why it is acceptable or how to manage it.
MissingThe choice is presented as if it costs nothing (fine: this is an extra).
Conclusion: So what now?a main move

End with your decision and a next step or condition. No new arguments. e.g. We therefore recommend a mixed model, with a review after six months.

With a next stepThe decision with a next step, a condition or a review (AE: how far the evidence supports the thesis, and what it can't show).
PresentThe decision is restated, but with no next step, condition or limit.
MissingThe text just stops, or ends on a new argument.

Every move is about thinking: finding the best source, the strongest objection and your answer to it. Planning matters as much as language.

3A structure that works

Put your recommendation first, give one paragraph to each decision, then face the objection. This order covers every part of the brief and puts each move where your readers expect it.

This is a guide, not a template. If your memo is close to it (two paragraphs merged, a part a little longer or shorter, the objection answered inside a decision paragraph), you lose nothing, as long as your readers can follow it and every part of the task is there.

1. Recommendationabout 40 words
Both decisions in one or two sentences, plus the main reason. Your readers should know what you want after reading this alone.
2. Decision 1about 60 words
Topic sentence with your choice. Then two pieces of evidence, each credited (As the finance director points out…), with numbers, and what they mean for the company.
3. Decision 2about 60 words
The same pattern, and how this decision affects the first one (Commission works best for people who…, which is why…).
4. Objection and riskabout 60 words
The strongest argument against your plan, said fairly and with its source, then your answer. Name the risk that remains and how to manage it.
5. Next stepsabout 30 words
One or two concrete actions: who does what, by when, or what to check first.

A few tips:

  • Skip the memo header. No To, From or Subject lines: what is marked is the body of your text, so spend your time there.
  • Headings are optional. Short paragraph headings (Recommendation, Risks) are fine in a memo. Bullet points are fine for next steps, but not for the argument itself.
  • Write to your readers. Use you and we, and speak to each manager's priority (Matthias, I know the cost worries you…). When the two readers disagree, show you have heard both.
  • Do the maths for them. Managers decide on numbers: turn the figures in the paper into costs, savings, payback times or risks.
  • Don't retell the story. Your readers know their own company. Every sentence should help them decide.

4Using your 45 minutes

Spend about ten minutes planning before you write a sentence. A planned 250 words beats an unplanned 350 on almost every move.

  1. Before the writing part. The situation at the start names the two questions. While you do the listening and reading, mark facts, numbers and opinions that bear on them, and note who said each one.
  2. Minutes 0–10: decide and plan. For each decision, jot down the two or three strongest facts for A and for B, with their sources. Do any sums you need. Choose. Write down how the two decisions affect each other, and the strongest objection to your plan.
  3. Minutes 10–35: write. Follow the structure above. Write the recommendation paragraph first. If you run short of time, it is the part that matters most.
  4. Minutes 35–45: check. Go through the checklist at the end of this guide, fix grammar and spelling, and check the word count.

5A model memo

This example is invented and much simpler than a real paper, but it shows the shape. The other options could be argued just as well: the grade comes from how the case is made, not from the choice.

The situation: Brakel Pumps, a Dutch maker of industrial pumps with 25 sellers, wants to grow its sales. Decision 1: A, pay the sales team partly on commission; B, keep fixed salaries. Decision 2: A, fill five open sales posts through a new training academy for graduates; B, hire experienced sellers. The readers are Ottilie Bellini (CEO) and Matthias Roeder (finance director). The "paper" says that sales have been flat at about €40 million for three years (radio report); that Sjoerd, head of sales, thinks experienced sellers expect commission and trainees would earn little at first, and Matthias fears costs will rise (meeting); that firms in the industry that pay commission sell about 12% more, but lose 30% of their staff a year when pay is mainly commission (article); and that the academy would cost €400,000 a year and take 18 months to produce its first sellers, while five experienced sellers would cost about €100,000 a year more than graduates (email from Ximena, HR).

I recommend paying our sales team partly on commission (Decision 1, A) and filling the five open posts with experienced sellers rather than through a training academy (Decision 2, B).

Our sales have been flat at about €40 million for three years, as the radio report points out, while competitors are growing. According to the article, firms in our industry that pay commission sell about 12% more. A mix of 70% fixed salary and 30% commission would reward the sellers who bring in new customers without putting everyone's income at risk.

The two decisions depend on each other. Commission works best for people who can sell from day one. Sjoerd made this point in the meeting: experienced sellers expect commission, while trainees would earn very little in their first year and might leave. Ximena's email adds that the academy would take 18 months to produce its first sellers, which is too slow if we want growth now.

Matthias's objection is the strongest one: commission and experienced staff will raise our costs. However, the five experienced sellers would cost about €100,000 a year more than new graduates, while the academy would cost €400,000 a year, and commission is only paid on sales we actually make. The remaining risk is staff turnover, which the article puts at 30% in firms that pay mainly by commission. Keeping most of the salary fixed should limit this, and we should review it after a year.

As next steps, I suggest that Ximena advertises the five posts this month and that Matthias models the 70/30 pay scheme.

261 words. Why it would get a grade in the 1 range:

Position
Both decisions in the first sentence.
Reasoning
Tied to the company's goal: growth now, from people who can sell from day one.
Evidence (the best source)
The figures that decide it, each credited: flat sales of €40 million (radio report), 12% more sales with commission (article), and the academy's 18 months (Ximena).
Counter-argument (the strongest one)
Matthias's worry about costs: the strongest point against the plan, put fairly and named.
Response
Answered with figures: €100,000 against €400,000 a year, and commission paid only on real sales.
Source check and trade-off
The 30% turnover in firms that pay mainly by commission is the price; the 70/30 split and a review after a year limit it.
Conclusion
Next steps with names: Ximena advertises the posts, Matthias models the pay scheme.

6Useful language and common mistakes

RecommendI recommend…On balance, the stronger option is…I would advise against…
Report a sourceAs Matthias points out, …According to the article, …In her email, Ximena warns that…
Use numbersThis costs us about… a year…while… would cost…This outweighs…
Link the decisionsThe two decisions belong together.If we choose A, then…This also affects…
Set conditionsprovided that…as long as…only if…
Face the objectionThe strongest argument against this is…This is a fair concern, but…To limit this risk, we could…
Be careful with claimsis likely tocouldmightsuggests that

The mistakes that hold a text back most:

  • Summarising instead of recommending. Retelling the recordings in order, then giving an opinion at the end.
  • Two separate answers. Treating the decisions as if they had nothing to do with each other.
  • Numbers without meaning. "Sales are flat at €40 million." So what? Say what it means for the decision.
  • Only one source. Using just the emails and forgetting the recordings and the article.
  • Taking one manager's side and ignoring the other. Your two readers often want different things: show you have heard both.
  • Mentioning the objection without answering it. "Some people may disagree" is not an answer.
  • Copying sentences from the paper, or chatty style. Report in your own words, and keep it professional: no honestly, a lot of, super important or exclamation marks.
