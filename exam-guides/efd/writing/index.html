Writing exam: how to get a good mark

English for Design · B2/C1

1The task

The writing part is one quarter of your exam (with listening, reading and speaking), and it gets one overall grade. You have about 45 minutes. You make two A/B decisions and write a recommendation memo of about 250 words to two named people at the company.

  • The decisions: two questions, each with option A or B. There is no single right answer. Your grade is for how well you argue, not for the options you pick: any choice you state clearly and give good reasons for gets credit.
  • Your readers: two people from the story, named in the task. They know their company and their product well. They need a clear decision and good reasons, not a summary of the paper.
  • Your material: everything else in the paper: the three recordings, the article and the two emails. All the facts, numbers and opinions you need are there.
  • The instructions always ask you to: make a clear recommendation on both decisions and explain how they affect each other; use information from the recordings, the article and the emails, including the numbers; answer the strongest objection to your plan; and write for your two readers, keeping their concerns in mind.

Aim for about 250 words. The word count itself isn't marked. But if your memo is much shorter, part of the task is probably missing, and if it is much longer, you are probably summarising.

2How it is marked

Your memo gets one overall grade, judged as university English at B2 (the level English for Design is graded at). The examiner reads the whole text and asks how well it does the task. Nothing is added up.

  • The grade comes from your argument moves. The examiner looks for eight moves (below): Position, Reasoning, Evidence, Counter-argument, Response and Conclusion, plus two extras, Source check and Trade-off. Each move reaches a level. For evidence and the other side: your own reasoning is good, a source from the paper is better, the best source in the paper is best. The six main moves done well are a 1,0; the two extras can make up for a gap elsewhere.
  • It's a language exam, so your choice isn't marked. There is no correct combination of options, and you aren't graded on business, design or subject knowledge. What counts is how well you communicate and argue: in English class, two plus two is five is good English; to and too be foar is bad English. So you get credit when you state your choice clearly and make a reasonable attempt to justify it from the paper. Either option can reach the top grade: the best source and the strongest objection exist for both sides.
  • Report the paper accurately. Getting a figure, a date or what someone said wrong is a weakness, because reading and reporting are part of the task. So is a reason that doesn't follow from what you cite.
  • The word count isn't marked. About 250 words is simply the length that lets you cover the task. A shorter text is only weaker if it leaves out something the task asks for.
  • At B2, what matters most is that your reader can follow your recommendation and your reasons. Mistakes are fine as long as they don't get in the way. You don't need to write above B2.
  • Language costs one step at most. Errors only lower the grade when they sometimes hide what you mean, and then by one step (for example 1,3 to 1,7). Small slips cost nothing.

1,0 · 1,3 · 1,7
A clear, competent answer to the task: the normal grade for good work. A clear, well-organised text that reports its sources accurately, with only small slips, gets 1,0 or 1,3; with some noticeable language errors, about 1,3 or 1,7. A 1,0 doesn't need a perfect text.
2,0 · 2,3 · 2,7

How your moves become the grade. All six main moves at their top level (a clear position, reasons tied to the case, the best source, the strongest objection answered, a conclusion with a next step) is a 1,0. A clear text that uses sources from the paper and answers a real objection is about a 1,3 or 1,7. The same text built only on your own reasoning, with no sources, is about a 2,3. A source check or a trade-off can lift you a step. Language errors cost one step at most, and only when they sometimes hide the meaning.

A clear, competent memo is in the 1 range. It doesn't have to be perfect: clear and well organised, with your sources reported accurately and only small slips, is a 1,0 or 1,3; with some noticeable language errors, about a 1,3 or 1,7. In about 250 words you can't include everything: a few well-chosen facts, with their sources, and the main objection answered in one or two sentences are enough. These are the same eight moves as in the feedback you get after every simulation (it shows the level each of your moves reached), and as in Write It Well (extras.simlearn.online/exam-writing/), so every sim is practice for the exam.

Position: What do you think?a main move

Say your choice clearly, near the start, so the reader knows what you want them to decide or do. e.g. Vossberg should keep at least one staffed till in every city store.

ClearThe choice is clear and comes early (in a whole report: a clear choice on each decision).
UnclearThere is a choice, but it comes late, is hedged until it means little, or doesn't say what to do.
MissingNo choice is stated, or it can't be found.
Reasoning: Why?a main move

Explain why your choice serves the goal: the logic that links it to what the reader cares about. e.g. City stores depend on regular older customers, who are the most likely to leave if a store feels impersonal.

Linked to the caseA reason tied to this case and its goal (these customers, this deadline, this risk).
GeneralA reason that would fit any case ('change is risky', 'it is modern'), or one that doesn't really follow.
MissingNo reason is given: the choice is only stated or repeated.
Evidence: What is it based on?a main move

Back your reason with facts or figures from the texts, and say who they come from. e.g. According to the finance director, basket value fell by 6% during the pilot.

The best sourceThe most decisive evidence the texts give for the writer's side (the key's 'best' evidence), reported accurately, with who said it.
A sourceA fact from the texts, reported accurately, but not the most decisive one available.
Own reasoningSupport from the writer's own ideas or general knowledge, not from the texts.
MissingNothing supports the choice.
Source check: How far can we trust it?an extra

Say in a clause how much weight a piece of evidence can carry: a small sample, the wrong season, a source with an interest, old data, one case only. e.g. That figure needs care, however, because the pilot ran over the summer.

SpecificA specific weakness of a specific source, taken from the texts (who has an interest, how small, how old, when).
GeneralA general doubt ('this may not be reliable') without saying why.
MissingEvidence is used without any comment on how far to trust it (fine: this is an extra).
Counter-argument: What does the other side say?a main move

Give the strongest point against your choice, fairly, and say who makes it. e.g. The operations manager argues that full self-checkout would save €2.1 million a year.

The strongest oneThe strongest objection the texts raise against the writer's side (the key's), stated fairly, with who makes it.
From a sourceA real objection from the texts, but not the strongest one against the writer's side.
Own ideaAn objection from the writer's own head, or a weak or generic one ('some people may disagree').
MissingThe other side doesn't appear.
Response: Why does your view still win?a main move

Answer the counter-argument: admit what is true in it, then show its limit with a reason or a fact. e.g. This is a real saving, but it assumes no extra losses from theft.

AnsweredThe true part is admitted and its limit shown with a reason or a fact from the texts.
AssertedAn answer, but only an opinion ('I still think…'), with no reason or fact.
MissingThe counter-argument is left unanswered, dismissed ('they are wrong') or attacks the person.
Trade-off: What does it cost?an extra

Name what your choice gives up, and why that price is worth paying. e.g. Keeping one staffed till means giving up a third of the savings, but that protects our most frequent customers.

Named and justifiedThe cost is named and the writer says why it is worth paying or how to limit it.
MentionedA cost or risk is mentioned, but not why it is acceptable or how to manage it.
MissingThe choice is presented as if it costs nothing (fine: this is an extra).
Conclusion: So what now?a main move

End with your decision and a next step or condition. No new arguments. e.g. We therefore recommend a mixed model, with a review after six months.

With a next stepThe decision with a next step, a condition or a review (AE: how far the evidence supports the thesis, and what it can't show).
PresentThe decision is restated, but with no next step, condition or limit.
MissingThe text just stops, or ends on a new argument.

Every move is about thinking: finding the best source, the strongest objection and your answer to it. Good planning matters as much as good English.

3A structure that works

Put your recommendation first. Then write one paragraph for each decision, and then answer the objection. This order covers the whole task and does most of the work for Organisation and flow.

This is a guide, not a template. If your memo is close to it (two paragraphs joined, one part a little longer or shorter), you lose nothing, as long as your readers can follow it and every part of the task is there.

1. Recommendationabout 40 words
Both decisions in one or two sentences, and your main reason. Your readers should know what you want after this paragraph.
2. Decision 1about 60 words
First sentence: your choice. Then two pieces of evidence, with numbers, and who they come from (In the interview, the buyer explained that…). Then what this means for the company.
3. Decision 2about 60 words
The same pattern, and how this decision affects the first one (The packaging decision depends on where we sell.).
4. Objection and riskabout 60 words
The strongest argument against your plan, with its source. Then your answer. Then the risk that is left and how to deal with it.
5. Next stepsabout 30 words
One or two clear actions: who does what, and when.

A few tips:

  • Skip the memo header. No To, From or Subject lines: what is marked is the body of your text, so spend your time there.
  • Headings are optional. Short headings (Recommendation, Risks) are fine. Bullet points are fine for next steps, but not for your argument.
  • Write to your readers. Use you and we. Show you know what worries them (Emre, I know you think customers want to see the product…).
  • Think like a designer and like a business. Your readers care about how the product looks and feels, and about what it costs. Use both.
  • Don't retell the story. Your readers know their company. Every sentence should help them decide.

4Using your 45 minutes

Plan for about ten minutes before you start writing. A planned memo of 250 words is better than an unplanned memo of 350 words.

  1. Before the writing part. The situation at the start tells you the two questions. During the listening and reading, mark the facts, numbers and opinions that are important for them, and write down who said each one.
  2. Minutes 0–10: decide and plan. For each decision, note the two or three best facts for A and for B, with their sources. Choose. Write down how the two decisions affect each other, and the strongest objection to your plan.
  3. Minutes 10–35: write. Use the structure above. Write the recommendation paragraph first. If you run out of time, it is the most important part.
  4. Minutes 35–45: check. Use the checklist at the end of this guide. Correct grammar and spelling, and count your words.

5A model memo

This example is invented and much simpler than a real paper, but it shows the shape. The other options could be argued just as well: the grade comes from how the case is made, not from the choice.

The situation: Bramble, a small Berlin lighting studio, is launching a new table lamp. Decision 1: A, sell through a chain of design stores; B, sell online only. Decision 2: A, a plain box in recycled cardboard; B, a colourful printed box. The readers are Ayla Dvořák (founder) and Emre Aksoy (sales). The "paper" says that the store chain takes 45% of the price, while online Bramble pays only for shipping (radio interview with a store buyer); that Emre thinks customers want to see and touch a lamp before they buy it (meeting); that in Ayla's survey, seven in ten customers said sustainability matters to them (meeting); that the plain box costs €2.10 and the printed box €3.80 (email from the production manager); and that colourful packaging helps products stand out on shop shelves (article).

We recommend selling the new lamp online only (Decision 1, B) and packing it in the plain recycled box (Decision 2, A). Together, these choices keep our costs low and match what our customers care about.

Selling online lets us keep more of the price. In the radio interview, the store buyer explained that the chain takes 45% of the price of each lamp. Online, we keep the full price apart from shipping. That money can go into better photos and product videos, which are our real shop window online.

The packaging decision depends on where we sell. On a shop shelf, a colourful box helps a product stand out, as the article shows. Online, the customer has already decided before they see the box. Then it has two jobs: to protect the lamp and to feel good when it is opened. The plain box does both and costs €2.10 instead of €3.80, as the production manager's email shows. It also fits our customers: in Ayla's survey, seven in ten said sustainability matters to them.

The strongest objection is Emre's: people like to see and touch a lamp before they buy it. This is a fair point, but we can answer it with two showroom weekends a year in our Berlin studio. The risk is that online sales grow slowly. To manage this, we could test the lamp in one design store next year and compare the results.

As a next step, we suggest ordering 500 plain boxes and planning the first showroom weekend for the spring.

About 260 words. Why it would get a grade in the 1 range:

Position
Both decisions in the first sentence.
Reasoning
Tied to the users and the brand: low costs, and what customers care about.
Evidence (the best source)
The decisive facts, each credited: the chain's 45% (store buyer), €2.10 against €3.80 (production manager), seven in ten care about sustainability (Ayla's survey).
Counter-argument (the strongest one)
Emre's point that people want to see and touch a lamp first: the strongest objection to selling online, put fairly.
Response
This is a fair point, but…, answered with a real idea: two showroom weekends a year.
Source check
The article's point about colourful boxes is weighed for where it applies: on a shop shelf, not online.
Trade-off
The risk that online sales grow slowly, with a plan to limit it: a test in one design store.
Conclusion
A next step: order 500 plain boxes and plan the first showroom weekend.

6Useful language and common mistakes

RecommendWe recommend…The better option is…We would not advise…
Say where a fact comes fromAs Emre says, …According to the article, …In her email, X explains that…
Say what a fact meansThis means that…As a result, …That money can go into…
Link the decisionsThe two decisions depend on each other.If we choose A, then…This also affects…
Set conditionsas long as…only if…provided that…
Answer the objectionThe strongest argument against this is…This is a fair point, but…To manage this risk, we could…
Be careful with claimsis likely tocouldmayprobably

The mistakes that hold a text back most:

  • Summarising instead of recommending. Retelling the recordings one by one, then giving an opinion at the end.
  • Two separate answers. Writing about the decisions as if they had nothing to do with each other.
  • Numbers without meaning. "The box costs €3.80." So what? Say what it means for the company.
  • Only one source. Using only the emails and forgetting the recordings and the article.
  • Describing the design but not deciding. Your readers need a choice and reasons, not a description of the product.
  • Mentioning the objection without answering it. "Some people may disagree" is not an answer.
  • Copying sentences or a chatty style. Use your own words. Avoid honestly, a lot of, super nice and exclamation marks.
