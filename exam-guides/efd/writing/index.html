Writing exam: how to get a good mark

English for Design · B2/C1

The taskHow it is markedA structure that worksUsing your 45 minutesA model memoUseful language and common mistakesFinal checklist

1The task

The writing part is worth 25 of your 100 exam points (the written paper is 75, the speaking exam 25). You have about 45 minutes. You make two A/B decisions and write a recommendation memo of about 250 words to two named people at the company.

  • The decisions: two questions, each with option A or B. There is no single right answer. You get marks for how well you argue, not for the options you pick.
  • Your readers: two people from the story, named in the task. They know their company and their product well. They need a clear decision and good reasons, not a summary of the paper.
  • Your material: everything else in the paper: the three recordings, the article and the two emails. All the facts, numbers and opinions you need are there.
  • The instructions always ask you to: make a clear recommendation on both decisions and explain how they affect each other; use information from the recordings, the article and the emails, including the numbers; answer the strongest objection to your plan; and write for your two readers, keeping their concerns in mind.

Aim for about 250 words. If your memo is much shorter, part of the task is probably missing. If it is much longer, you are probably summarising.

2How it is marked

The memo is marked on five criteria, each out of 5 points, judged at the course level (B2/C1). You don't need to write above that level. Grades use the usual scale: 95 per cent of the points for a 1,0, 90 per cent for a 1,3, and so on in steps of five down to 50 per cent for a 4,0. For the 25 writing points, that means:

These are the same five criteria as in the feedback you get after every simulation. It rates each one Exam-ready (full marks), Nearly there (4) or Not yet (3 or less), and its tips follow this guide, so every sim is practice for the exam.

Task and reader5 points

Give a clear answer to the brief, written for your reader.

Full marks (5)The recommendation is clear from the start and every part of the brief is covered in proportion. The text is written for the named reader throughout: the right format, level of detail and tone.
2–3 pointsThere is a recommendation, but it comes late or is hedged, or one part of the brief is missing. The text reads more like a summary of the sources than a document for this reader.
Using the information5 points

Choose, combine and report the information that decides the question.

Full marks (5)Uses the decisive information from across the sources (in a sim, the group's), accurately and in the writer's own words, and combines sources to make points none of them makes alone. Key facts are credited (who said it) and linked to what they mean for the decision.
2–3 pointsUses some relevant information, but relies on one or two sources, lists facts rather than using them, or copies wording closely; a key fact is missing or slightly wrong.
Building the argument5 points

Make a convincing case: reasons, evidence and consequences, with the other side answered.

Full marks (5)A convincing, well-prioritised case: each main point is supported by evidence and its consequences are spelled out; the strongest counter-argument or trade-off is acknowledged and answered; the remaining risk is named with a way to manage it.
2–3 pointsRelevant points, but often asserted rather than supported, or all treated as equally important; the other side is ignored or only mentioned.
Organisation and flow5 points

Guide your reader through the text.

Full marks (5)Logically structured for its purpose; each paragraph develops one idea; varied, natural linking shows how ideas relate, so the reader never has to work out the connection.
2–3 pointsA recognisable structure, but paragraphs mix ideas or the order makes the reader work; linking is limited or sometimes misused.
Language5 points

Say exactly what you mean, accurately and professionally.

Full marks (5)A range that lets the writer say exactly what they mean, used precisely and naturally for the course's target level; errors are rare and minor; the style is consistently professional.
2–3 pointsEnough range for the main points, but sometimes imprecise or repetitive; noticeable errors that occasionally make the reader pause; some slips in style.

Three criteria are about thinking (task, information, argument). Good planning matters as much as good English.

3A structure that works

Put your recommendation first. Then write one paragraph for each decision, and then answer the objection. This order covers the whole task and gives you most of the organisation marks.

1. Recommendationabout 40 words
Both decisions in one or two sentences, and your main reason. Your readers should know what you want after this paragraph.
2. Decision 1about 60 words
First sentence: your choice. Then two pieces of evidence, with numbers, and who they come from (In the interview, the buyer explained that…). Then what this means for the company.
3. Decision 2about 60 words
The same pattern, and how this decision affects the first one (The packaging decision depends on where we sell.).
4. Objection and riskabout 60 words
The strongest argument against your plan, with its source. Then your answer. Then the risk that is left and how to deal with it.
5. Next stepsabout 30 words
One or two clear actions: who does what, and when.

A few tips:

  • Skip the memo header. No To, From or Subject lines: what is marked is the body of your text, so spend your time there.
  • Headings are optional. Short headings (Recommendation, Risks) are fine. Bullet points are fine for next steps, but not for your argument.
  • Write to your readers. Use you and we. Show you know what worries them (Emre, I know you think customers want to see the product…).
  • Think like a designer and like a business. Your readers care about how the product looks and feels, and about what it costs. Use both.
  • Don't retell the story. Your readers know their company. Every sentence should help them decide.

4Using your 45 minutes

Plan for about ten minutes before you start writing. A planned memo of 250 words gets more marks than an unplanned memo of 350 words.

  1. Before the writing part. The situation at the start tells you the two questions. During the listening and reading, mark the facts, numbers and opinions that are important for them, and write down who said each one.
  2. Minutes 0–10: decide and plan. For each decision, note the two or three best facts for A and for B, with their sources. Choose. Write down how the two decisions affect each other, and the strongest objection to your plan.
  3. Minutes 10–35: write. Use the structure above. Write the recommendation paragraph first. If you run out of time, it is the most important part.
  4. Minutes 35–45: check. Use the checklist at the end of this guide. Correct grammar and spelling, and count your words.

5A model memo

This example is invented and much simpler than a real paper, but it shows the shape.

The situation: Bramble, a small Berlin lighting studio, is launching a new table lamp. Decision 1: A, sell through a chain of design stores; B, sell online only. Decision 2: A, a plain box in recycled cardboard; B, a colourful printed box. The readers are Ayla Dvořák (founder) and Emre Aksoy (sales). The "paper" says that the store chain takes 45% of the price, while online Bramble pays only for shipping (radio interview with a store buyer); that Emre thinks customers want to see and touch a lamp before they buy it (meeting); that in Ayla's survey, seven in ten customers said sustainability matters to them (meeting); that the plain box costs €2.10 and the printed box €3.80 (email from the production manager); and that colourful packaging helps products stand out on shop shelves (article).

We recommend selling the new lamp online only (Decision 1, B) and packing it in the plain recycled box (Decision 2, A). Together, these choices keep our costs low and match what our customers care about.

Selling online lets us keep more of the price. In the radio interview, the store buyer explained that the chain takes 45% of the price of each lamp. Online, we keep the full price apart from shipping. That money can go into better photos and product videos, which are our real shop window online.

The packaging decision depends on where we sell. On a shop shelf, a colourful box helps a product stand out, as the article shows. Online, the customer has already decided before they see the box. Then it has two jobs: to protect the lamp and to feel good when it is opened. The plain box does both and costs €2.10 instead of €3.80, as the production manager's email shows. It also fits our customers: in Ayla's survey, seven in ten said sustainability matters to them.

The strongest objection is Emre's: people like to see and touch a lamp before they buy it. This is a fair point, but we can answer it with two showroom weekends a year in our Berlin studio. The risk is that online sales grow slowly. To manage this, we could test the lamp in one design store next year and compare the results.

As a next step, we suggest ordering 500 plain boxes and planning the first showroom weekend for the spring.

About 260 words. Why it would score well:

Task and reader
Both decisions in the first sentence. It answers Emre's worry directly and uses Ayla's survey.
Using the information
Facts from the interview, the article, the meeting and the email, each with its source. Each fact is followed by what it means (That money can go into…). The article and the email are put together to explain why the box choice depends on where Bramble sells.
Building the argument
Point, evidence, result in each paragraph. Emre's objection is put fairly and answered with a real idea (showroom weekends). The risk that is left has a plan (a test in one store).
Organisation and flow
One idea per paragraph, each with a clear first sentence. Linking words show the logic: Together, Then, It also, but, To manage this.
Language
Clear design and business words (shop window, stand out, sustainability, showroom). Careful claims (could test, grow slowly). Simple, correct sentences in a professional style.

6Useful language and common mistakes

RecommendWe recommend…The better option is…We would not advise…
Say where a fact comes fromAs Emre says, …According to the article, …In her email, X explains that…
Say what a fact meansThis means that…As a result, …That money can go into…
Link the decisionsThe two decisions depend on each other.If we choose A, then…This also affects…
Set conditionsas long as…only if…provided that…
Answer the objectionThe strongest argument against this is…This is a fair point, but…To manage this risk, we could…
Be careful with claimsis likely tocouldmayprobably

The mistakes that cost the most marks:

  • Summarising instead of recommending. Retelling the recordings one by one, then giving an opinion at the end.
  • Two separate answers. Writing about the decisions as if they had nothing to do with each other.
  • Numbers without meaning. "The box costs €3.80." So what? Say what it means for the company.
  • Only one source. Using only the emails and forgetting the recordings and the article.
  • Describing the design but not deciding. Your readers need a choice and reasons, not a description of the product.
  • Mentioning the objection without answering it. "Some people may disagree" is not an answer.
  • Copying sentences or a chatty style. Use your own words. Avoid honestly, a lot of, super nice and exclamation marks.
