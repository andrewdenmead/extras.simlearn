Writing exam: how to get a good mark

Academic English · C2

1The task

The writing part is one quarter of your exam (with listening, reading and speaking), and it gets one overall grade. In about 45 minutes you write a research-based argumentative essay of about 300 words (300–350 is fine) on the essay question printed in bold above the instructions.

  • The question always joins two A/B decisions. Your thesis must take a position on both, and say how they affect each other.
  • Your reader: an academic reader who is well informed about the debate and sceptical, but not part of the review. There is no addressee: this is an essay, not a memo or a policy brief.
  • Your role: step out of your role in the story and write as an independent researcher.
  • Your sources: the three recordings, the article and the two briefing notes. You bring them together (synthesise them) and cite them.
  • The instructions always ask you to: end the introduction with a thesis on both decisions and your main reason; combine evidence from more than one source in each body paragraph, including the numbers; cite every source in one simple, consistent style; put the strongest counterargument fairly, with its source, and rebut it; and end with a hedged conclusion.

The genre box on the page sums up what a strong essay does. Read it before you plan.

2How it is marked

Your essay gets one overall grade, judged as university English at C2 (the course level). The examiner reads the whole text and asks how well it does the task. Nothing is added up.

  • The five criteria are labels, not points. Task and reader, Using the information, Building the argument, Organisation and flow and Language (below) each get Exam-ready, Nearly there or Not yet. They show you what to work on next. The grade is one judgement of the whole text, so a Nearly there or two can still be in the 1 range.
  • It's a language exam, so your choice isn't marked. There is no correct combination of options, and you aren't graded on business, design or subject knowledge. What counts is how well you communicate and argue: in English class, two plus two is five is good English; to and too be foar is bad English. So you get credit when you state your choice clearly and make a reasonable attempt to justify it from the paper. You don't need the best possible source, argument or counterargument.
  • Report the paper accurately. Getting a figure, a date or what someone said wrong is a weakness, because reading and reporting are part of the task. So is a reason that doesn't follow from what you cite.
  • The word count isn't marked. About 300 words is simply the length that lets you cover the task. A shorter text is only weaker if it leaves out something the task asks for.
  • At C2 (Academic English has the highest expectations), you need control of the academic genre, of using sources and of organisation: a thesis, synthesis and consistent citations, in an academic style. You are a developing academic writer: nobody expects publication-ready prose.
  • No deduction for every slip. One missing detail or a few language errors don't each cost you something: what counts is how much the weaknesses limit the text as a whole.

A clear, competent essay is in the 1 range. It doesn't have to be perfect: clear and well organised, with your sources reported accurately and only small slips, is a 1,0 or 1,3; with some noticeable language errors, about a 1,3 or 1,7. In about 300 words you can't include everything: a few well-chosen findings, with their sources, and the strongest counterargument answered in two or three sentences are enough. These are the same five criteria and labels as in the feedback you get after every simulation, and its tips follow this guide, so every sim is practice for the exam.

Task and readera label, not points

Give a clear answer to the question, written as an argumentative essay for an academic reader.

Exam-readyA clear thesis on both decisions at the end of the introduction, and every part of the task is covered in proportion. The text works as the genre asked for throughout, for an academic reader: the expected structure, consistent citations and an academic register.
Not yetThere is a position, but it comes late, is too vague to argue with or covers only one decision, or one part of the task is missing. The text reads more like a summary of the paper than an argument for an academic reader. At its weakest: no clear position, or most of the task is not addressed; the genre and purpose are unclear.
Using the informationa label, not points

Choose, combine and report the information that decides the question.

Exam-readyUses the decisive information from across the sources (in a sim, the group's), accurately and in the writer's own words, and combines sources to make points none of them makes alone. Key facts are credited (who said it) and linked to what they mean for the decision.
Not yetUses some relevant information, but relies on one or two sources, lists facts rather than using them, or copies wording closely; a key fact is missing or slightly wrong. At its weakest: little information from the simulation, or it is inaccurate, uncredited or irrelevant to the decision.
Building the argumenta label, not points

Make a convincing case: reasons, evidence and consequences, with the other side answered.

Exam-readyA convincing, well-prioritised case: each main point is supported by evidence and its consequences are spelled out; the strongest counter-argument or trade-off is acknowledged and answered; the conclusion says how far the evidence supports the thesis and what it cannot yet show.
Not yetRelevant points, but often asserted rather than supported, or all treated as equally important; the other side is ignored or only mentioned. At its weakest: little reasoning: opinions or a list of choices without support, or points that contradict each other.
Organisation and flowa label, not points

Guide your reader through the text.

Exam-readyLogically structured for its purpose; each paragraph develops one idea; varied, natural linking shows how ideas relate, so the reader never has to work out the connection.
Not yetA recognisable structure, but paragraphs mix ideas or the order makes the reader work; linking is limited or sometimes misused. At its weakest: little structure: a block of text or a list of disconnected points.
Languagea label, not points

Say exactly what you mean, accurately and in an academic style.

Exam-readyA range that lets the writer say exactly what they mean, used precisely and naturally for the course's target level; errors are rare and minor; the style is consistently academic.
Not yetEnough range for the main points, but sometimes imprecise or repetitive; noticeable errors that occasionally make the reader pause; some slips in style. At its weakest: a limited range for the task; frequent errors that make parts hard to follow.

In Building the argument, the step from Nearly there to Exam-ready usually comes down to two things: a counterargument that is only mentioned rather than answered, and a conclusion that over-claims.

3The structure of the essay

Four paragraphs: an introduction, two body paragraphs, a conclusion. This is the structure the genre box asks for, and it leaves enough room in 300 words for real synthesis.

This is a guide, not a template. If your essay is close to it (three body paragraphs instead of two, the counterargument inside a body paragraph, a part a little longer or shorter), you lose nothing, as long as your reader can follow the argument and every part of the task is there.

Introductionabout 60 words
The issue and why it matters, in one or two sentences, then your thesis as the last sentence: a position on both decisions and your main reason. A reader should be able to disagree with it.
Body 1: your main claimabout 100 words
A topic sentence that states one claim. Evidence combined from at least two sources, with numbers and citations. Reasoning that links it back to the thesis, and shows how the two decisions affect each other.
Body 2: the counterargumentabout 100 words
The strongest case against your position, put at its strongest and cited. Then your rebuttal, with evidence from another source. Concede what is true in it.
Conclusionabout 60 words
Your thesis in new words. How far the evidence supports it. What the evidence cannot yet show (its limits) and, if useful, what would test it.

A few tips:

  • No headings, no bullet points. It is an essay: continuous prose in paragraphs.
  • Synthesis, not a tour of the sources. Organise paragraphs around claims, not around sources ("The article says… The radio report says…").
  • The decisions are linked. Say how your answer to one shapes the other (this does not compete with…; it is what makes… work).
  • No new material in the conclusion. It weighs what you have already shown.

4Citing the sources

Cite every source you use, in one simple style, and name it exactly as the paper does. The sources have no dates, so don't invent any. The instructions give examples in this form:

A briefing note
(Briefing note 1)
The article
('Title of the article', para. 4)
A person in a recording
Surname (committee hearing), or as Surname argues in the committee hearing: name the recording as the paper does
The news report
(radio report)
  • Cite claims, figures and positions, not just quotations. A paraphrased number still needs a citation.
  • Give the paragraph number for the article: it shows you used the source precisely.
  • Keep it consistent. Choosing one style and sticking to it counts for more than which style you choose.
  • Quote sparingly. A short phrase in quotation marks is fine when the wording matters. Otherwise, paraphrase.

5Using your 45 minutes

Spend about ten minutes planning before you write a sentence. In an argumentative essay, the plan is the argument.

  1. Before the writing part. The situation at the start names the two questions. While you do the listening and reading, mark evidence on both sides, with its source (the article's paragraph numbers, the speaker's name).
  2. Minutes 0–10: decide and plan. Write your thesis in one sentence. Choose the claim for body 1 and the two sources you will combine for it. Find the strongest counterargument, its source, and the evidence that answers it. Note one limit of the evidence for the conclusion.
  3. Minutes 10–35: write. Introduction, body 1, body 2, conclusion. Cite as you go: adding citations afterwards is slow and leads to mistakes.
  4. Minutes 35–45: check. Go through the checklist at the end of this guide, check citations for consistency, fix grammar and spelling, and check the word count.

6A model essay

This example is invented and much simpler than a real paper, but it shows the shape. The other options could be argued just as well: the grade comes from how the case is made, not from the choice.

The question: Should Halbridge introduce a levy on overnight stays, and should the money go into local services or into promoting the city? Decision 1: A, introduce the levy; B, don't. Decision 2: A, spend the revenue on services residents share with visitors (street cleaning, buses, parks); B, spend it on promoting the city in the quiet months. The sources: a radio report, a council meeting (with Oyelaran, a councillor), the article 'Paying to stay?' and two briefing notes.

Few local policies divide a tourist city as sharply as charging visitors for an overnight stay. Supporters see a fair contribution to the services tourists use; hoteliers see a tax that will drive visitors elsewhere. This essay argues that Halbridge should introduce the levy (Decision 1) and spend it on the services residents share with visitors rather than on promoting the city (Decision 2), because a levy is only fair if residents can see where the money goes.

The evidence that a modest levy does not deter visitors is strong. Briefing note 1 reports that overnight stays in comparable cities fell by less than 2 per cent in the first year, and the radio report notes that most visitors interviewed did not know what they had paid. Yet acceptance depends on how the money is used. The article finds that where the revenue disappeared into general budgets or marketing, residents' support fell within three years ('Paying to stay?', para. 6). Spending it on visible local services therefore does not compete with the levy; it is what keeps the levy legitimate.

The strongest objection is that small guesthouses will absorb the cost. Oyelaran (council meeting) points out that many family-run businesses work on thin margins and cannot pass the charge on, and argues that promoting the city in the quiet months would at least bring them more guests. This concern is serious, but the radio report suggests that promotion mainly fills the larger hotels first. Briefing note 2 estimates that exempting businesses with fewer than five rooms would cost only 4 per cent of the revenue, which would address Oyelaran's case directly rather than compensate for it.

On balance, the sources suggest that a levy spent on shared services is more likely to be accepted, and to last, than one spent on promotion. However, the cities in Briefing note 1 are larger and busy all year, and none of the sources shows how Halbridge's seasonal visitors would respond. A levy with a fixed review date would test what this essay can only argue in principle.

About 345 words (300–350 is fine). Why it would get a grade in the 1 range:

Task and reader
The thesis ends the introduction, covers both decisions and gives the main reason. No addressee, an academic register, and a sceptical reader is assumed throughout.
Using the information
Body 1 combines a briefing note, the radio report and the article to make a point none of them makes alone (the levy lasts only if residents see the money). Every figure is cited in one style.
Building the argument
The counterargument is put at its strongest, with its source, then answered with evidence from another source. The two decisions are explicitly linked. The conclusion names the limits of the evidence.
Organisation and flow
Four paragraphs, each with one job and a clear topic sentence. Linking carries the logic: Yet, therefore, This concern is serious, but, On balance, However.
Language
Precise academic vocabulary (comparable cities, legitimate, compensate for, in principle). Hedging matches the evidence (suggest, more likely to). Consistently academic, never chatty.

7Useful language and common mistakes

Synthesise sourcesX reports…, and Y confirms…Taken together, these sources suggest…Yet these gains depend on…
Introduce a counterargumentThe strongest objection is that…Critics, notably X, argue that…
Concede and rebutThis concern is serious, but…While it is true that…, …This does not, however, show that…
Link the decisionsThis does not compete with…; it is what makes… work.The answer to the first question shapes the second.
Hedgesuggestsis more likely toappears toon balancethe evidence does not yet show

The mistakes that hold a text back most:

  • A thesis on only one decision, or one too vague to argue with ("There are good arguments on both sides").
  • Summarising the sources one by one instead of combining them around a claim.
  • Writing a memo or policy brief. No Dear…, no recommendations addressed to a committee, no headings.
  • Missing or inconsistent citations, or invented dates.
  • Mentioning the counterargument without answering it, or choosing a weak one to knock down easily.
  • An over-confident conclusion. "This proves that…" claims more than any of the sources can show.
  • Slips in register. Contractions, a lot of, huge, rhetorical questions, and I think in place of an argued claim.
