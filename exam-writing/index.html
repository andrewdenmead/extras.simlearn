Write It Well

A 45-minute workshop on the building blocks of a strong exam text: position, reasoning, evidence, checking sources, the other side, trade-offs and a clear conclusion.

3 min

What the examiner is looking for

Your exam text is not a test of what you know about business, design or politics. It's a test of whether you can argue clearly in English. The examiner asks one big question:

Could a busy reader act on this? Do they know what you think, why you think it, what it's based on, what the other side says, and what it will cost?

What earns the grade

  • A clear position early on
  • Reasons that explain why, not just that
  • Evidence from the texts, with the source named
  • A fair look at the other side, and an answer to it
  • An honest trade-off and a conclusion that follows

What matters less than you think

  • The "right" answer. Either choice is fine if you justify it well.
  • Perfect grammar. Clear reasoning beats fancy language.
  • Headers and layout. To/From/Subject lines don't earn points. The body does.
  • A fixed template. Close enough to the structure is fine.

How the grade works: good, better, best

The examiner looks for the eight moves you'll meet today. Each one can be done at different levels. For your evidence and for the other side:

GoodYour own reasoning or general knowledge: "Older people often prefer talking to staff."
BetterA source from the texts, with who said it: "The store manager says queues were shorter."
BestThe strongest source in the texts for your point: "In the pilot, basket value fell by 6%, according to the finance director."
How it adds up: six main moves (position, reasoning, evidence, counter-argument, response, conclusion) all done at their best level = 1,0. A clear text with sources from the texts and a real objection answered = about 1,3–1,7. The same text built only on your own reasoning = about 2,3. Two extras (source check, trade-off) fill in where a main move is weaker: they're optional, but they can lift a text whose main moves aren't all at their best. Language mistakes cost one step at most, and only when they hide what you mean.

Tap each card to see the move, an example, useful phrases and its levels, from good to best. Every good exam text uses most of these moves, though not always in this order and not always one sentence each. The six main moves carry your grade; the two extras can fill in where a main move is weaker.

You don't have to use the extras: leaving them out costs nothing. But if one of your main moves is weaker (say your evidence is your own reasoning rather than a source, or your conclusion has no next step), a source check or a trade-off earns points that make up for it. A text with all six main moves at their best is a 1,0 even without the extras.
Register tip. Aim for clear and professional, not stiff. Write as you would to a manager you respect. Business memos can be quite direct ("We recommend…"). Academic essays avoid contractions and "I think", and use hedging ("this suggests", "it appears").

Don't just use the numbers: check them

Exam texts are full of claims and figures. Strong writers use them and say how far to trust them. What is the main weakness in each claim?

Remember: a weak source isn't useless. You can still use it, as long as you tell the reader how much weight it can carry. One short clause does the job: "…although the sample was small." A general doubt ("this may not be reliable") is good; naming the exact weakness, as you did here, is best.

Name the price of your choice

Every decision costs something. Good writers say what they are giving up and why it is worth it. Weak writers pretend their option has no downside. Pick the strongest trade-off sentence.

A trade-off has three parts: what you gain · what you give up · why it's worth it.
X means giving up Y, but…The cost of this is…, which we accept because…This comes at the price of…On balance, … outweighs …While this sacrifices…, it protects…

Take this into the exam

Screenshot this or keep the page open while you do the next sim.

  1. Say what you think, early. Your reader should know your position after one or two sentences.
  2. Explain why. Link each point back to the goal: customers, money, risk, people.
  3. Use the texts, and name who said it. "According to the Finance Director…" is worth more than "Some people say…".
  4. Check the evidence in one clause. Small sample? Interested party? Wrong season? Old data? One case only?
  5. Give the other side its best shot, then answer it. Agree with the part that's true, then show why your view still wins.
  6. Name the price. What does your option cost, and why is that acceptable?
  7. End with a decision and a next step. A review date, a condition, a pilot. No new arguments in the conclusion.
