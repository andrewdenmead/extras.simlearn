The Jar Auction

Before class

One jar (or bag) of beads, sold to the highest bidder. The class is usually right on average, but the winner is usually wrong. Ten to fifteen minutes.

1. Fill the jars

Count 200–300 beads into jar A. For round 2, use a second jar with a different number, or refill the same jar while the class discusses round 1.

The counts stay hidden (dots) and are kept on this computer only, so you can type them in before the projector is on.

2. Print the slips for round 2

Half the class gets an analyst note with a number that's far too high; the other half gets no note. Students write their round 2 bid on the slip they get, so the two groups sort themselves.

Make the estimate about 1.5 to 2 times jar B's real count.

3. On the day

Blank slips for round 1, both jars ready, and one student with a phone calculator. You type the bids in on this screen; the page does the sums.

Round 1

The auction

“I'm selling this jar. Every bead in it is worth one euro. Write down the most you'd pay for it, and your name. The highest bid wins the jar and pays exactly what they bid. No counting out loud, no conferring.”

Pass the jar round, or walk it past each table: about a minute of looking in total. Collect the slips.

Type in the bids

Separate numbers with spaces or new lines. A name in front is optional.

    Debrief

    So who really won?

    1. Winner of round 1: how did it feel when the count came out? Why does the person who wins an auction so often lose?
    2. The class average was close to the real number. So why was the winning bid so far off?
    3. Did the analyst's estimate move your bid? Did you notice it moving you?
    4. A company selling its shares on the stock market for the first time sets a price. The investors who want the most shares are often the most optimistic. Should it price high to take advantage of that, or price lower so the people who buy make money and the share price rises?
    5. When a company sells shares, who plays the role of the analyst note? Who puts a confident number into everyone's head?
    The analyst doesn't exist. The estimate was made up, to see how far one confident, unchecked number could move the room.
