The Jar Auction

On paper · 10–15 minutes · before a sim on financial markets or share pricing

At a glance

  • The point: students feel the winner's curse (the person who wins an auction is usually the one who overestimated most) and anchoring (a confident number pulls everyone's judgement), just before they price something themselves.
  • You need: a clear jar or bag of beads (two if you can, or refill one), slips of paper, the board, and one student with a phone calculator.
  • It can't stall: everyone writes a bid at the same time. Nobody has to speak first.

Before class (5 minutes)

  1. Jar A: count 200–300 beads into it (e.g. 247). Write the number on a card and keep it in your pocket.
  2. Jar B (round 2): a different count, e.g. 190. With only one jar, refill it with a different number while the class discusses round 1.
  3. Analyst slips: for half the class, slips that say “Analyst note: experts estimate this jar holds around 400 beads.” Make the estimate clearly too high (about 1.5–2 times the real count). For the other half, slips that say “No analyst note this round.” Students write their round 2 bid on the slip they get, so the slips sort themselves afterwards.
  4. Blank slips for round 1.

Round 1 · The auction (5 minutes)

“I'm selling this jar. Every bead in it is worth one euro. Write down the most you'd pay for it, and your name. The highest bid wins the jar and pays exactly what they bid. No counting out loud, no conferring.”
  1. Pass the jar round or walk it past each table: about a minute of looking in total.
  2. Collect the slips.
  3. Your calculator student works out the class average while you write the top five bids on the board.
  4. Announce the winner, then reveal the real count.
  5. Winner's result = real count − their bid. Write it up.

What usually happens: the class average lands surprisingly close to the real count, but the winning bid is well above it. The winner “won”, and lost money.

Round 2 · The analyst note (5 minutes)

  1. Hand out the analyst slips face down. Students read their own slip silently.
  2. Show jar B. Same rules: write your bid on the slip you were given.
  3. Collect and sort into two piles: analyst note and no note.
  4. The calculator student works out each pile's average. Write both on the board.
  5. Reveal the real count. Don't explain the gap yet: let them notice it.

Debrief (5 minutes)

  1. Winner of round 1: how did it feel when the count came out? Why does the person who wins an auction so often lose?
  2. The class average was close to the truth. So why was the winning bid so far off?
  3. Analyst-note group: did the estimate move your bid? Did you notice it moving you?
  4. Bridge: a company selling its shares on the stock market for the first time has to set a price. The investors who want the most shares are often the most optimistic. Should it price at the top of the range to take advantage of that, or price lower so buyers make money and the share price rises after listing?
  5. When a company sells shares, who plays the role of the analyst note? (The banks running the deal, analysts, media hype, a famous investor buying in.)

Reveal last: the “experts” in round 2 don't exist. The number was made up, to see how far one confident, unchecked figure could move the room.

Language to pre-teach

TermMeaningExample
bid (n./v.)the price a buyer offersMy bid was €260.
valuationwhat something is judged to be worthThe bank's valuation looked far too optimistic.
overpay / overvaluepay more than something is worthThe winner overpaid by €40.
the winner's cursewinning because you overestimated the mostWinning the jar was a classic case of the winner's curse.
anchoringbeing pulled towards a number you heard firstThe analyst's figure anchored everyone's bids.
price rangethe band a share price will be set within; the top of the range is its upper endThey priced at the top of the range.
oversubscribedmore demand than there are shares on offerThe share sale was four times oversubscribed.
leave money on the tablesell for less than you could have gotPricing low leaves money on the table.
first-day popa sharp rise in the share price on the first day of tradingA first-day pop pleases investors but means the price was set too low.

If things go off script

  • The winner didn't overpay: “Lucky this time. But look at the top five: how many were above the real count?” The point still stands.
  • Big group (25+): students bid in pairs. Faster to collect, and pairs have to argue their number, which is extra speaking.
  • Someone tries to count every bead: hold the jar at the front instead and cap looking time at a minute.
  • Short on time: skip round 2 and keep debrief questions 1, 2 and 4.
